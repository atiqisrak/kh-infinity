# BSTI Approval for Imported Food: The 56-Product List Most Importers Find Out About at the Port Gate

There is a particular category of bad news in import operations: the kind that arrives after your shipment has already left origin. You have committed capital to the purchase order. The goods are on the water. And someone — your importer, your customs agent, or a Chattogram port officer — tells you that your product requires BSTI conformity assessment, and that assessment was not arranged before the vessel sailed. What follows is weeks of port detention, accumulating charges, and an anxious wait for a sampling outcome that should have been managed months earlier.

This happens regularly in Bangladesh food imports. Not because the requirement is obscure — BSTI has published the mandatory conformity assessment list — but because many importers either don't know it applies to their product category or don't manage it at the sourcing stage. The 56-product mandate is among the most consequential compliance requirements in Bangladesh's food import framework, and the cost of discovering it late is measurable in weeks and thousands of dollars.

## What BSTI Conformity Assessment Actually Means

Bangladesh Standards and Testing Institution (BSTI) is the national body responsible for setting and enforcing Bangladesh Standards (BDS) across industries. For imported goods, BSTI's mandatory conformity assessment scheme requires that specified products — including a substantial list of food categories — conform to Bangladesh Standards before they can be cleared through customs.

In practice, conformity assessment for imported food takes two forms:

**Pre-shipment CAB route:** The overseas manufacturer is assessed by a Bangladesh-recognized Conformity Assessment Body (CAB) before the shipment departs. The CAB conducts a factory audit and/or product testing at origin and issues a conformity certificate recognized by BSTI. This process typically takes 30-90 days, depending on the CAB's schedule and the complexity of the product.

**Port-of-entry sampling route:** For shipments that arrive without a pre-shipment CAB certificate, BSTI may conduct sampling at Chattogram port. Samples are collected from the container, sent to a BSTI laboratory, and the results determine whether the consignment can be cleared. Laboratory turnaround adds 2-4 weeks to the clearance timeline — during which the container sits in the port yard, accumulating demurrage charges.

The pre-shipment route is predictable and time-bounded. The port-sampling route is neither. Importers who don't manage BSTI compliance at the sourcing stage are, by default, choosing the port-sampling route — without having consciously made that choice.

## Which Food Categories Are in Scope

The BSTI mandatory conformity list covers an extensive range of imported food categories. The following are representative of the categories most relevant to Bangladesh's food import market — and to KH Infinity's product range:

**Edible oils and fats:** Sunflower oil, soybean oil, palm oil, and refined vegetable oils all fall under BSTI conformity assessment requirements. For a market as significant as Bangladesh's edible oil import segment, this is not a niche compliance edge case — it applies to every commercial-scale oil import.

**Processed dairy products:** Skimmed milk powder, full-cream milk powder, and processed dairy imports require BSTI conformity. Dairy products carry additional complexity because they also fall under BFSA oversight — a point addressed further below.

**Refined sugar and confectionery:** Imported sugar and sugar-based products are in scope.

**Biscuits and bakery products:** Packaged bakery imports from any origin require conformity assessment.

**Fruit juices and beverages:** Imported packaged juices and non-alcoholic beverages fall under the scheme.

**Sauces, condiments, and seasonings:** This category includes soy sauce, fish sauce, and similar condiments — all of which are active import categories in Bangladesh.

**Packaged spices:** Cumin, coriander, chili, and similar packaged spice imports require conformity assessment.

**Pulses and legumes (packaged):** Chickpeas and lentils in packaged form are included.

This list is representative, not exhaustive. The full mandatory schedule covers over 56 food product categories. If you are importing any packaged or processed food product into Bangladesh, the working assumption should be that BSTI conformity applies — and the verification step should happen at the purchase order stage.

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong

The financial exposure from a BSTI-unmanaged food shipment is not hypothetical. Consider a standard scenario:

A 20-foot container of imported soy sauce arrives at Chattogram port. The importer has not arranged pre-shipment BSTI CAB certification. The container enters port-of-entry sampling. BSTI collects samples and submits them to the laboratory. Testing takes 14-21 working days. During this period, port demurrage charges accumulate at the applicable CPA rate.

The direct cost in port charges for a 21-day hold on a 20-foot container runs USD 800-2,000 depending on shipping line tariff and CPA yard fees. This is before any duty dispute, before any re-examination, and before any consideration of the opportunity cost — the food manufacturer waiting on their raw material, the distributor who has committed to a delivery date, the retail buyer who has placed orders.

For perishable or semi-perishable food products — including certain processed foods, dairy, and products with limited shelf life — a 3-4 week port hold is not just a financial cost. It can render the entire consignment commercially unusable by the time it is released.

## BSTI vs. BFSA: Two Different Requirements, Both Mandatory

A critical point that trips up even experienced food importers: BSTI and BFSA are separate regulatory requirements, and passing one does not satisfy the other.

**BSTI** is concerned with whether the product conforms to Bangladesh Standards — defined characteristics, labeling requirements, composition thresholds. A cooking oil must meet the BDS specification for that oil type. A packaged spice must meet the BDS labeling and composition requirements for spices.

**BFSA (Bangladesh Food Safety Authority)** is concerned with food safety — microbiological contamination levels, pesticide residues, food additives, and general food safety parameters defined under the Food Safety Act 2013.

A cooking oil can conform to BSTI's BDS standard but fail BFSA's food safety parameters if it contains elevated levels of a restricted additive or contaminant. Conversely, a product can pass BFSA inspection but fail BSTI conformity if it doesn't meet labeling or composition specifications under the relevant BDS.

Both requirements must be managed. They have different timelines, different approval bodies, and different compliance processes. An importer who manages one and not the other has done half the work and created all the risk.

## The Sourcing-Stage vs. Port-Stage Distinction

The core problem with BSTI non-compliance is not that the requirement is difficult to meet. It is that the compliance process has a minimum lead time — 30-90 days for pre-shipment CAB certification — that must be initiated before the purchase order is finalized, not after.

By the time a shipment is at sea, the window for CAB pre-certification has closed. The importer's only options at that point are port-of-entry sampling (with the associated timeline uncertainty) or, in some cases, returning the cargo to origin — which is rarely a commercially viable option.

This is why BSTI compliance is a sourcing-stage function, not a clearance-stage function. An importer who manages BSTI at the clearance stage is structurally committed to the port-sampling route. An importer who manages it at the sourcing stage — identifying the applicable BDS standard, engaging a recognized CAB, and building the CAB timeline into the overall supply chain schedule — delivers food cargo that clears Chattogram on time, every time.

## What Procurement Managers Should Be Asking

If you are procuring food commodities for import into Bangladesh, the following questions should be part of your importer evaluation:

- Which BSTI standards apply to this specific product, and what is the relevant BDS number?
- Have you worked with a recognized CAB for pre-shipment certification on this product category?
- What is the current CAB timeline for pre-shipment certification, and how does that integrate with our purchase order timeline?
- How do you manage BFSA requirements for this product alongside BSTI?

An importer who can answer these questions with specificity — citing actual BDS numbers, named CABs, and documented timelines — is managing BSTI at the sourcing stage. One who hedges or defers the question is one who will discover the requirement at Chattogram port.
