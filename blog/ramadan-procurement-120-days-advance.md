# Ramadan Procurement: The Importers Who Win Book 120 Days Out. Here's Why.

Every year, Bangladesh's food import market experiences the most predictable supply shock in South Asia. Every year, a significant portion of the import community treats it as a surprise. The importers who win Ramadan are not smarter than the importers who scramble — they are simply earlier. The 120-day rule is not a heuristic. It is arithmetic derived from supply-chain lead times, commodity price curves, and Chattogram port congestion patterns that repeat annually without meaningful variation.

## The Demand Reality: What Ramadan Does to Bangladesh's Food Market

Bangladesh's Ramadan consumption pattern is among the most pronounced in the world. The country's 160 million Muslims observe Ramadan with extraordinary food intensity — Iftar spreads, Sehri preparations, and a full month of home cooking that is categorically more ingredient-intensive than the rest of the year.

The import demand uplift, measured against non-Ramadan monthly averages:

**Dates: +300%.** Bangladesh imports 100,000–150,000 MT of dates annually. An estimated 70–80% of that annual volume arrives and is consumed within 30 Ramadan days. This is not a gradual seasonal increase — it is a demand cliff that begins the day before Ramadan and sustains for exactly one month. Jordan Medjool, Saudi Ajwa, Tunisian Deglet Noor — every variety experiences the same spike. The implication: 70–80% of the annual date supply chain must be operational and in-country before Ramadan's first week.

**Sugar: +40%.** Iftar sweets, mithai, sherbet, and jalebi drive a sharp consumption surge. Bangladesh's confectionery and food service sector, already importing ICUMSA 45 refined sugar from Brazil, sees a demand wave that suppliers in São Paulo begin tracking six months in advance.

**Cooking oil: +25%.** Iftar frying — piaju, beguni, aloo chop, pakoda — is among the most oil-intensive cooking traditions in South Asia. Sunflower oil and palm oil consumption rises sharply from Ramadan's first day.

**Cumin: +60%.** Essential for Ramadan cooking. Used in everything from biryani to Iftar snacks. The demand spike for jeera during Ramadan is among the sharpest of any single spice.

## The Price Curve: What Timing Actually Costs

Commodity price behaviour on Ramadan-sensitive products follows predictable seasonal curves. Understanding those curves transforms the timing decision from a scheduling preference into a financial calculation.

**Jordan Medjool dates — the price curve:**

- **October–November:** Pre-harvest pricing. Limited new-crop supply available. Prices at seasonal baseline but stable.
- **December:** Fresh harvest arrives from Jordan's Wadi Araba valley. Supply at seasonal peak. This is the single best buying window of the year — new-crop quality, maximum shelf life, lowest prices of the cycle.
- **January–February:** Bangladesh's entire importer community enters the market simultaneously. Demand overwhelms available spot supply. Prices rise 20–40% above December levels. Importers who waited now pay a significant premium for the same product they could have contracted in December.
- **March (30 days pre-Ramadan):** Remaining available stock — whatever hasn't already been contracted — is priced at 40–60% above the December baseline. Any importer still sourcing at this point is paying for their procrastination.

The importer who contracted Jordan Medjool in December and the importer who buys spot in February are receiving the same product. The February buyer is paying 20–40% more. On a 100 MT order, the price difference can be $15,000–$30,000 at recent Medjool prices — for the same product, the same origin, the same specification.

**Brazil ICUMSA 45 sugar — the supply cycle:**

Brazil's sugarcane harvest runs April through November. December through March is the supply-tightening period — global buyers are competing for inventory that won't be replenished until the next harvest cycle begins. Bangladesh importers entering the sugar market in January and February find prices 15–25% above their October–November equivalents.

The structural issue: every Bangladeshi food importer needs Ramadan sugar. Every Indian food importer needs Eid/festival sugar. Every Middle Eastern trader is also positioning. The January–February global demand concentration pushes prices against every buyer simultaneously.

**Cumin — the Unjha market dynamic:**

Indian cumin from Unjha, Gujarat is the primary supply source for Bangladesh. The Indian domestic market also experiences Ramadan-related demand. An importer who books cumin in October is working with surplus from the previous harvest cycle — prices are stable, quantity is available. An importer who books in January is competing with Indian domestic demand on a commodity where supply is fixed until the next Rajasthan harvest.

## The Chattogram Congestion Trap: Why Timing Isn't Just About Price

Even an importer who accepts the higher January–February commodity prices faces a second compounding problem: Chattogram port and BFSA processing during pre-Ramadan season.

February and March are the port's highest-traffic months for food commodity arrivals. Every Bangladesh food importer has converged on the same logic: ship in February, clear in late February or early March, distribute before Ramadan. The result is entirely predictable:

**Port congestion:** Container vessels arriving simultaneously fill berths and stack yards. Containers wait for inspection appointments that are booked out two weeks.

**BFSA inspector capacity:** Bangladesh Food Safety Authority inspectors are at capacity during peak arrival periods. A lab test that normally takes 5–7 business days takes 15–20 business days when the queue is full. An importer whose vessel arrives February 15 — expecting to distribute by February 25 — may not clear until March 10. Ramadan begins March 20.

**BSTI testing:** Products requiring BSTI quality certification face similar queue extension. A December arrival clears with standard 5–10 business day processing. A February arrival competes with 50 other containers for the same inspectors.

**Demurrage accumulation:** Containers sitting at port waiting for clearance accumulate demurrage charges. At Chattogram's standard rates, 3–4 weeks of demurrage on a 40-foot container is BDT 80,000–120,000 ($700–1,100). An importer with five containers facing extended congestion clearance has absorbed BDT 400,000–600,000 in port charges alone — on top of the higher commodity prices they paid for late booking.

## The 120-Day Calculation

The arithmetic is straightforward once lead times are mapped.

**For a March Ramadan (2027 example):**

- Ramadan begins: approximately March 10, 2027
- Desired in-warehouse date: February 20 (to allow two weeks of distribution)
- Chattogram clearance time (normal, pre-congestion): 7–12 business days
- Required arrival at Chattogram: February 5
- Sea transit from origin (Jordan/UAE for dates, Brazil for sugar, India for cumin): 18–25 days
- Required departure from origin: January 10–15
- Supplier booking, packing, documentation lead time: 4–6 weeks
- Order placement deadline: **November–December**

An importer who places orders in October and November — 120–150 days before Ramadan — departs origin in late November or December, arrives Chattogram in January, clears in January at normal processing speed, warehouses in late January, and distributes through February at zero congestion. They paid December commodity prices, cleared at standard BFSA timelines, and incurred zero demurrage.

An importer who places orders in January is beginning where the October importer finished.

## The Full Ramadan Basket

The commodities that move on this cycle are not independent procurement decisions. They are a basket:

- **Dates** (Jordan Medjool, Saudi Ajwa): Middle East corridor, cold chain, 18–22 days
- **Sugar** (Brazil ICUMSA 45): Atlantic corridor, 28–35 days transit
- **Cumin** (India Unjha): short transit, 5–7 days, but Unjha market dynamics demand early booking
- **Cooking oil** (Ukraine/Argentina sunflower): long-haul corridor, 30–40 days transit

Each commodity has its own supply-chain lead time, its own price curve, and its own BFSA documentation requirements. An importer managing all four simultaneously — coordinating four different supplier relationships, four sets of origin documentation, four vessel bookings — faces logistics complexity that rewards operational experience.

The importers who consistently win Ramadan are not improvising. They are executing a procurement sequence they have run before, with supplier relationships that allow December booking at reliable prices, and logistics partners who can consolidate multi-commodity shipments without documentation errors.

## One Season to Understand, Every Season to Execute

The Ramadan procurement cycle is fully legible to any experienced food commodity importer. The price curve, the congestion pattern, the lead times — none of this is hidden information. What separates the importers who execute it profitably from those who scramble is operational infrastructure: supplier relationships that can be activated in October, carrier relationships that secure reefer space before the market tightens, and customs clearance capability that processes documentation efficiently even when the port is busy.

That infrastructure takes multiple seasons to build. For importers who are scaling into this market, partnering with an operator who has already built it is the rational first-mover strategy.
