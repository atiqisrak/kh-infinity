# Why Bangladesh Phone Repair Shops Keep Getting Burned by Fake 'Grade A' Displays

Every phone display supplier in Bangladesh's component market calls their product Grade A. This is not an exaggeration — it is a market-wide observation. Walk Elephant Road's two kilometres of repair shops and component dealers, ask any supplier what grade they stock, and the answer is Grade A. Walk into a Khatunganj shop in Chittagong and the answer is the same. The problem is not that Grade A claims are being made about bad product. The problem is that "Grade A" means nothing without specification — and most suppliers are relying on that meaninglessness.

## The Unregulated Label

Grade A is not a regulated product classification. No government body in Bangladesh, India, or China defines it. No accreditation body audits for it. No standard document specifies what measurements must be met to qualify. It is a commercial term that any supplier can apply to any product without technical basis or legal consequence.

This creates a structural problem for repair shop owners. When every supplier claims Grade A and no supplier provides measurable specification documentation, the buyer's only recourse is to install the display, return the phone to the customer, and wait to see what happens. If the display fails — wrong brightness, poor colour accuracy, touch response problems, True Tone incompatibility — the workshop finds out when the customer returns. By then, the display has been installed and the supplier has been paid.

Bangladesh's phone repair market concentrates this problem. Elephant Road Dhaka hosts more than 2,000 repair shops and component dealers in a dense corridor. Screen damage represents 60 to 65 percent of all phone repairs. iPhone 11 and iPhone 12 are the most common models at the repair level, given their prevalence in Bangladesh's BDT 15,000 to 20,000 second-hand smartphone market. The volume of display replacements is enormous. The proportion of that volume delivered under a label that means nothing is similarly enormous.

## What Genuine Grade A OLED Actually Means

Grade A as a meaningful specification is not a mystery. It has measurable, testable parameters. The problem is that most suppliers do not specify these parameters — either because they cannot meet them or because specifying them would invite scrutiny they cannot withstand.

**Panel substrate identity** is the foundational criterion. Genuine Grade A OLED for iPhone replacement uses Samsung Display Corporation (SDC) or BOE OLED panels — the same substrate manufacturers that supply Apple's original assembly. These panels are identifiable through spectrophotometric analysis. A Grade A claim backed by a verifiable panel substrate declaration is materially different from a Grade A claim backed by nothing. Grade B and Grade C panels use panels from lower-tier manufacturers with inferior colour accuracy, brightness uniformity, and long-term stability.

**Brightness** is measurable with a calibrated meter. Genuine Grade A displays for iPhone 12 deliver 800 nits or above in typical brightness. Standard panels (sometimes called Grade A- in honest markets) measure 700 to 800 nits. Grade B panels measure 500 to 650 nits. The difference is visible to any user who takes their phone outdoors or uses it in a bright environment — which in Bangladesh's climate is most of the year. A customer whose iPhone 12 screen was replaced three weeks ago and whose display now looks dim in sunlight will return to the workshop. They have no obligation to believe it is a parts problem rather than a service problem.

**Colour gamut** matters for iPhones iPhone 12 and above. Apple's display pipeline uses P3 wide colour coverage, which reproduces a significantly larger colour space than the older sRGB standard. An iPhone 12 display replacement that covers sRGB only — which many Grade B units do — displays colours with less saturation and accuracy than the original display. The user perceives this as the phone looking wrong after repair. For a workshop that charges BDT 3,000 to 5,000 for a display replacement, perception that the phone looks wrong is not a minor complaint.

**Touch sampling rate** must match the original specification. An iPhone 12 replacement display must deliver 60Hz touch response; certain models require 120Hz. A mismatch produces touch latency — the display feels sluggish compared to the original. This is one of the most common workshop complaint triggers and one of the most frequently attributed to installation error rather than component quality, which means the workshop absorbs the reputational cost of a parts problem.

**True Tone compatibility** is specific to iPhone 11 and above. Apple's True Tone system uses ambient light sensors to adjust display warmth in real time. The replacement display must include the True Tone IC — a small integrated circuit embedded in the display flex cable — for the feature to function. Grade B displays frequently omit this IC entirely or use a cloned version that fails to communicate correctly with the iPhone's system. The customer loses True Tone functionality after the repair. This is observable immediately and requires explanation from the workshop.

**CE and RoHS certification** are the documentary baseline for responsible electronics sourcing. CE certification confirms that the display assembly meets European electromagnetic compatibility standards. RoHS certification confirms that it does not contain prohibited hazardous substances above threshold concentrations. These certificates are issued by accredited testing laboratories. They are verifiable. A supplier who can produce them has shipped product through a documented quality chain. A supplier who cannot has not.

## The Real Cost of a Grade B Display Sold as Grade A

The economics of installing a substandard display are clearer when the full cost is calculated rather than the unit purchase price.

A repair shop charges BDT 3,000 to 5,000 to replace an iPhone 12 display. If the installed display is 550 nits (Grade B) instead of 800 nits (Grade A), the customer observes the difference within their normal use. They return. The workshop now faces a choice: argue that the display is functioning correctly, which damages the customer relationship, or install a replacement display at cost. The replacement costs the workshop another panel (BDT 2,000 to 3,000 at informal market prices), another 30 to 45 minutes of technician time, and the customer's confidence.

At a 10 percent callback rate — conservative for informal-sourced Grade B displays — a workshop doing 50 iPhone screen repairs per month faces five redos. Each redo costs BDT 2,500 to 4,000 in parts and labour. Monthly redo cost: BDT 12,500 to 20,000. Annual redo cost: BDT 150,000 to 240,000. This is cost that appears nowhere on a purchase invoice but is directly generated by component quality decisions.

The reputational cost does not have a clean monetary value but is not negligible. Bangladesh's phone repair market increasingly operates through word of mouth and Facebook recommendation. A customer who had a screen replacement fail and was poorly handled in the callback is a customer who tells others. The workshop that builds a reputation for using documented, warranted components builds a referral base. The workshop that does not builds a complaint base.

## The Models That Drive Bangladesh's Repair Volume

Understanding where the volume concentrates helps a distributor or importer prioritise SKUs. iPhone 11 is the most common broken screen at Bangladesh's repair level — its prevalence in the BDT 15,000 to 20,000 second-hand market means a high-volume installed base. iPhone 12 is the growing middle tier. iPhone 13 sits at the premium end.

On Android — which accounts for 95 percent of Bangladesh's smartphone user base — Samsung Galaxy A14, A34, OPPO A57, Xiaomi Redmi Note 12, and Realme and Vivo equivalents drive the display replacement volume. These models' display assemblies require the same specification scrutiny: panel substrate, brightness, colour gamut, touch response. The Grade A versus Grade B distinction is as consequential here as on iPhone, though brand-specific certification documentation is structured differently.

A distributor serving Elephant Road workshops needs consistent SKU coverage across these active models, with documentation that a workshop owner can reference when a customer asks what grade of display was installed. That documentation does not exist in the informal supply chain. It is the differentiating product of a formal import programme.
