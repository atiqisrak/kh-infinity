# Elephant Road's Supply Chain Problem: Why Formal B2B Display Imports Are Replacing Courier Sourcing

Elephant Road in Dhaka is the nerve centre of Bangladesh's phone repair and component trade — more than 2,000 shops, dealers, and distributors concentrated in a stretch of road that supplies repair workshops across the country. The supply chain feeding this market has, until recently, run almost entirely through informal channels: personal courier, luggage trade, and grey market aggregators. That is changing, driven not by regulation alone but by simple economics. Formal B2B import is becoming competitive, and the informal route is becoming more expensive.

## How Elephant Road Currently Sources Phone Displays

Understanding why the supply chain is shifting requires understanding what it looks like today. Three channels dominate.

**Personal courier from Shenzhen** is the most common mechanism for mid-volume operators. A shop or dealer identifies a Shenzhen supplier through Alibaba or direct contact, places an order of 10 to 50 display units, and ships via DHL or FedEx. The parcel arrives in seven to ten days. At small quantities and declared values below NBR's duty threshold, the shipment clears without significant duty assessment. This is the channel that feels efficient, because it is fast and the upfront cost appears low.

The efficiency is partly an illusion. NBR's duty assessment on electronics parcels applies to declared values above BDT 17,000 — approximately $150. A 20-unit iPhone 12 display order at $12 per unit declares at $240 and sits above threshold. Duty at MFN rates plus VAT adds 25 to 40 percent to declared value. Many importers undervalue declarations to stay below the threshold, which creates seizure and penalty risk that materialises unpredictably. The channel's apparent cost advantage is partly a function of paying duties inconsistently — sometimes not at all, sometimes heavily, with no predictability.

**Luggage trade** is the oldest channel and the one with the most fully hidden costs. Traders travel the Dhaka-Bangkok-Guangzhou or Dhaka-Bangkok-Shenzhen route, acquire displays at Shenzhen market prices, pack them in checked luggage, and re-enter Bangladesh through Hazrat Shahjalal Airport. The unit purchase price in Shenzhen looks attractive. The effective per-unit cost, once flights, accommodation, agent fees, and the trader's margin are included, is 15 to 25 percent higher than the Shenzhen price. The product also arrives with zero documentation — no manufacturer identity, no CE certification, no warranty.

**Informal Dhaka aggregators** are the final layer of the informal chain. These dealers buy from luggage traders and courier importers, add their margin of 20 to 30 percent, and sell to Elephant Road shops and to workshop operators across Dhaka. By this point, the display has been through two or three informal hands. No documentation has been generated at any stage. The buyer at the end of this chain receives a product whose manufacturer, grade, and certification status are unknown, at a price that includes the margins of everyone who handled it before them.

## The NBR Tightening That Is Changing the Calculus

The informal channel's cost advantage has never been as large as it appeared, because it was always partly subsidised by inconsistent enforcement. That enforcement is tightening.

NBR's scrutiny of informal electronics imports has increased materially since 2023. BTRC and NBR have jointly documented counterfeit BTRC type-approval stickers on imported phone accessories — a specific enforcement focus that has increased examination rates on electronics parcels and made seizures more common. Importers who were accustomed to parcels clearing without examination now face higher rates of duty assessment and, in some cases, seizure.

The practical effect: the informal channel's effective cost is rising. Importers who previously relied on inconsistent enforcement are either paying duties they previously avoided or facing seizures that convert apparent savings into actual losses. Neither outcome serves the business.

Formal import is not subject to these enforcement risks because it is not attempting to avoid duty. A formal B2B import with correct HS classification, CE/RoHS documentation, and proper duty payment does not attract NBR scrutiny — it is precisely the kind of import that customs systems are designed to clear efficiently.

## The Economics of Formal vs. Informal at Volume

The comparison that matters for distributors is not between a 20-unit informal courier shipment and a formal import container. It is between informal sourcing at the volume a real distributor actually moves — and formal import at the same volume.

A Dhaka distributor supplying 20 to 30 repair shops needs, in a typical month, 100 to 200 display units across active iPhone and Android models. At that volume, the per-unit economics of informal and formal sourcing look quite different from what they appear at small scale.

**Informal courier per iPhone 12 Grade A OLED display**: Shenzhen spot price $12-14, DHL freight per unit $4-6, effective duty cost (when assessed, at MFN rates) $3-5 per unit, no warranty, no documentation. Effective landed cost per working unit: $19-25, with 10-15% defect rate absorbed as pure loss.

**Formal B2B import per iPhone 12 Grade A OLED at 100+ units**: CIF Chattogram $10-14 per unit (volume pricing vs. spot), container freight per unit $0.50-1.50, customs duty at applicable rate plus VAT, 90-day warranty on defects. Effective landed cost per working unit: $13-19 at current duty rates, with warranty coverage reducing defect loss to near zero.

At volumes above 100 units per model, the formal channel is cost-competitive or cheaper than the informal channel — and delivers documentation, warranty, and regulatory compliance that informal sourcing cannot provide at any price.

The crossover point does require minimum order coordination. Not every individual shop on Elephant Road can reach 100 units per model independently. But distributors — the tier of buyer that serves multiple shops — reach this volume regularly. And increasingly, distributors are recognising that pooling orders across shops, even from competitors, to reach formal import minimums is preferable to continuing to absorb the informal channel's hidden costs.

## The Distributor Consolidation Trend

Bangladesh's electronics component distribution is consolidating at the upper tier. The largest Elephant Road distributors — those supplying 50 or more repair shops across Dhaka and beyond — are moving to formal import, and the shift is being driven by three separate pressures simultaneously.

First, the economics, as described above, increasingly favour formal channels at the volumes these distributors move. Second, the regulatory environment is making informal sourcing less predictable. Third, the competitive differentiation available to distributors who can offer documented, warranted product is growing as larger repair chains and franchise operations enter Bangladesh's market and demand better supply chain credentials than the grey market provides.

The distributors who move to formal import first are capturing the cost and documentation advantage before their competitors. The ones who wait are continuing to accumulate the hidden costs of the informal channel while that channel's enforcement environment becomes less forgiving.

## What Changes With Formal B2B Import

A distributor who transitions to formal B2B import through a Shenzhen-sourced supplier like KHI gains: CE and RoHS certification documentation for every shipment, which is increasingly requested by repair chains and franchisees; a 90-day warranty on defective units, eliminating the cost of absorbing defect losses; correct HS classification, eliminating seizure risk; volume pricing that reflects genuine B2B rates rather than spot market prices; and supply chain traceability — the ability to tell a customer or regulator where the product came from.

What they give up: the flexibility of ordering 20 units at a time with seven-day delivery. This is a real trade-off. Formal import requires forward planning and minimum order coordination. Distributors who operate hand-to-mouth, restocking weekly from informal aggregators, need to shift their inventory management to make formal import work. That shift is an operational investment. For the distributors moving in this direction, the evidence is that it is worth making.
