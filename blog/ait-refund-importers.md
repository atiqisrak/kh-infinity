Every commercial import into Bangladesh attracts a 5% Advance Income Tax (AIT) collected at the port of entry by the National Board of Revenue. On a $100,000 CIF shipment, that is $5,000 paid upfront, before the goods have left the port, before a single unit has been sold.

In theory, AIT is not a permanent cost. It is an advance payment against the importer's corporate income tax liability — a prepayment of tax that should be credited against the annual tax return. In practice, most importers never see that money back.

The gap between theory and practice represents one of the larger hidden costs in Bangladesh's import ecosystem — and understanding why it persists reveals a great deal about what rigorous import management actually requires.

## What the Law Says

Under the Income Tax Ordinance 1984 (and the subsequent Income Tax Act 2023), AIT collected at the import stage is creditable against the importer's assessed corporate tax liability for the same tax year. If the AIT paid exceeds the assessed tax, the excess is refundable, or carryforward provisions apply.

The mechanism is clear. The path to recovery is not.

## Why Recovery Is Rare in Practice

**Condition 1: You must have a functioning corporate tax file.** AIT is linked to the importer's Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN). To claim the credit, the importer must have filed a corporate tax return for the year in question, showing income, expenses, and assessed tax. Companies that import regularly but file incomplete returns — or file late — lose the credit by default.

**Condition 2: The AIT must appear in the tax return with supporting documentation.** The customs duty payment receipt (the Treasury Challan) must accompany the return. Importers who cannot produce these documents for every shipment — particularly where multiple agents or brokers handled clearances — cannot substantiate the claim.

**Condition 3: The assessed tax must be sufficient to absorb the credit.** AIT is a credit against assessed tax, not a direct refund. If an importer's assessed tax for the year is lower than the AIT paid — a common situation for companies with low taxable profit, high expenses, or operating losses — the excess AIT sits as a recoverable amount. Recovering that amount as cash requires a formal refund application to the NBR, a process with significant administrative friction.

**Condition 4: The refund process itself.** Even when all documentation is correct and the refund is unambiguously due, NBR refund processing is not rapid. Companies regularly report 12-24 month delays on AIT refund applications. For a small or mid-size importer, this represents locked working capital at a meaningful scale.

## What It Actually Costs to Leave AIT Unrecovered

Consider a consistent importer bringing in 10 containers per year, each with a CIF value of $50,000. Total CIF: $500,000. AIT at 5%: $25,000 per year.

For a company that does not recover AIT, this $25,000 is a permanent annual cost layered on top of the standard duty burden. Over five years, that is $125,000 in unrecovered government levies — money that was legally creditable but never claimed.

At a 20% gross margin, $125,000 in lost tax credits is equivalent to losing the gross profit on $625,000 in sales. This is not a theoretical number. It is the real impact on many mid-size importers who treat AIT as a cost of business rather than a recoverable amount.

## The Categories Most Affected

AIT impact is highest in product categories where CIF values are large and margins are tight:

**Edible oils:** Single-container CIF values of $40,000-$80,000 mean AIT of $2,000-$4,000 per shipment. High-volume importers accumulate significant annual AIT exposure.

**Food commodities in bulk:** Skimmed milk powder, sugar, pulses — commodity-value shipments where the AIT on a single 20 MT container can exceed $3,000.

**Industrial materials:** Tarpaulin in commercial quantities, packaging materials, construction inputs. The AIT exposure scales directly with shipment value.

**Electronics and phone parts:** China-sourced displays, components, accessories. Higher unit values mean higher AIT on each consignment.

## What Recovery Actually Requires

An importer who genuinely intends to recover AIT needs four things working simultaneously:

**Clean TIN and regular corporate filings.** The company must maintain current corporate tax compliance — no missed filing years, no undisclosed income issues. AIT credits cannot be claimed by non-compliant taxpayers.

**Document custody for every shipment.** Every Treasury Challan from every import clearance must be preserved, matched to the corresponding Bill of Entry, and organized by tax year. This requires systematic record-keeping at the logistics and accounts level, not ad-hoc filing.

**Coordination between customs agent and tax advisor.** The person managing customs clearances and the person preparing the corporate tax return must be working from the same data. In most mid-size import businesses, these functions operate independently, and the reconciliation never happens.

**A realistic assessment of the refund path.** For companies where AIT consistently exceeds assessed tax — a common situation in early-stage or lower-profit importers — the credit carryforward or refund application route requires a dedicated tax advisory relationship. Without it, the theoretical credit remains unclaimed.

## What This Means for Import Cost Planning

Importers should model two scenarios when calculating landed cost:

**Scenario A — AIT irrecoverable:** Add AIT to the total duty stack. This gives the conservative, cash-flow-accurate landed cost for businesses that cannot confidently recover the levy.

**Scenario B — AIT recoverable:** Treat AIT as a timing cost (working capital tied up until the credit is applied) rather than a permanent cost. This requires the infrastructure described above to be in place and functional.

For most small and mid-size importers in Bangladesh, Scenario A is the realistic one. The ambition should be to build toward Scenario B — but not to assume it before the operational infrastructure supports it.

The importers who price their goods at Scenario B while their operations actually look like Scenario A are the ones who discover margin problems at year-end rather than at the purchase order stage.
