# Importing Phone Parts from China to Bangladesh: Why the Informal Route Is Getting More Expensive

The informal phone parts import route from China to Bangladesh was never as cheap as it appeared. It carried hidden costs — defect losses with no recourse, per-unit freight premiums at small scale, and inconsistent duty exposure — that did not appear on any invoice. What is changing now is that the enforcement environment is tightening, and the informal route's most important cost subsidy — avoiding duty payment reliably — is becoming unreliable. When that subsidy disappears, the formal route's economics look very different.

## The Apparent Logic of Informal Sourcing

The informal channel has a straightforward appeal. A repair shop or small distributor contacts a Shenzhen supplier, orders 20 iPhone 12 displays at $12 per unit, ships via DHL, and receives the parcel in seven days. No L/C, no bank documentation, no customs agent. The transaction is simple.

This simplicity is real. The channel genuinely offers speed and low transactional complexity. For a business that manages inventory week to week and cannot plan further ahead than the immediate order, it feels efficient. The question is whether what feels efficient is actually the lowest cost option — and whether its costs are stable over time.

## The Hidden Costs That Make Informal Sourcing Expensive

The informal route's real cost is substantially higher than the unit purchase price suggests. Each of the following costs is real, recurring, and frequently absent from the mental accounting that makes informal sourcing seem attractive.

**Duty at the parcel stage** is the first hidden cost, and the most variable. NBR duty on electronics parcels applies when declared value exceeds approximately BDT 17,000 — roughly $150. A 20-display order at $12 declared value sits at $240, above the threshold. Customs duty at MFN rates for phone displays under HS 8524.91.00 is significant, and VAT applies on the cumulative duty-inclusive value. When assessment occurs, the added cost is 25 to 40 percent of declared value.

Many importers undervalue declarations to stay below the threshold. This is not without consequence. NBR's detection of undervaluation triggers duty assessment on the full assessed value, plus a penalty. Seizure removes the entire shipment from the importer's access — the goods are gone and the money paid to the supplier is gone with them. The informal channel's duty cost is not zero when assessment is avoided. It is a contingent cost: sometimes zero, sometimes a penalty, occasionally a seizure. That contingency does not appear in per-unit pricing but is a real component of per-unit cost over time.

**The defect rate premium** is the second major hidden cost. Shenzhen spot suppliers selling to individual buyers — small order quantities, no ongoing relationship, no quality control requirements from the buyer — do not segregate Grade A from Grade B product. What ships is whatever was available in the warehouse. Industry experience with informal sourcing channels puts the defect rate at 10 to 15 percent of units received. These are displays that fail shortly after installation — brightness below spec, touch sensor malfunction, connector failure, True Tone IC absence on iPhone models.

Each defective display absorbed as a loss represents the full cost of the unit plus the labour cost of the installation that had to be redone. If a distributor buys 100 displays at $12 per unit and 12 are defective, the effective cost per working unit is not $12. It is $12 × 100/88 = $13.64 per working unit — and that calculation assumes zero warranty on the working units, which is also true. Every defect loss comes 100 percent out of the buyer's margin.

**Per-unit logistics cost** is the third hidden cost, and it compounds with scale. DHL parcel freight for a 20-unit display shipment from Shenzhen to Dhaka runs approximately $3 to $6 per unit depending on weight and declared value. This cost is paid on every small order. A distributor who orders 20 units per week pays $3 to $6 per unit every week, 52 times per year. At formal container import scale, per-unit freight for the same product is $0.50 to $1.50 per unit. The difference — $2 to $5 per unit — on 1,000 units annually is $2,000 to $5,000 in direct freight premium, paid for the privilege of ordering in small quantities.

**Administrative overhead** is the fourth cost, and the least quantified. Coordinating multiple weekly courier orders — supplier contact, payment transfer, parcel tracking, receipt, quality checking — is work. For a distributor managing multiple product categories and multiple suppliers, the administrative cost of informal sourcing is real time spent by real staff. A quarterly formal import consolidates the same volume into a single procurement, logistics, and customs clearance cycle. The administrative saving is not trivial.

## The Formal Import Economics at Volume

At distribution volumes — 100 or more units per model per quarter — the formal import economics are straightforwardly competitive, often better than the informal channel's true all-in cost.

CIF Chattogram for Shenzhen-sourced iPhone display panels runs approximately $10 to $14 per unit at B2B volume pricing, depending on model and grade. Container freight from Shenzhen to Chattogram adds $0.50 to $1.50 per unit. Customs duty under HS 8524.91.00 at Bangladesh's applicable rates plus VAT produces a landed cost in the BDT 1,800 to 2,800 range per unit depending on the specific model and current duty schedule — with full duty payment, no undervaluation risk, and no seizure exposure.

Against that landed cost: 90-day warranty from delivery, eliminating defect loss. CE and RoHS certification, enabling supply to repair chains and franchise operations that request documentation. Correct HS classification, eliminating regulatory risk. Manufacturer traceability, enabling quality disputes to be resolved through a documented supply chain rather than absorbed as unrecoverable losses.

The informal channel's effective landed cost — purchase price plus DHL freight plus duty (when assessed) plus defect losses with no recovery — is, for many distributors, already higher than formal import. The formal channel is not an upgrade from cheap to expensive. For distributors at the right volume, it is a shift from expensive-and-undocumented to competitive-and-documented.

## The Crossover Point and How Distributors Are Reaching It

The formal channel requires minimum order coordination that the informal channel does not. A single shop ordering 20 units per month cannot easily absorb the minimum quantities that make container import cost-effective. A distributor serving 20 to 50 shops can.

The crossover point — where formal import becomes cost-competitive or better — sits at approximately 50 to 100 units per model per quarter. Distributors serving multiple workshops typically exceed this threshold on active models (iPhone 12, Samsung Galaxy A34, OPPO A57) without effort. For models with lower demand, order pooling across multiple distributors or shops is possible — and increasingly common among Elephant Road distributors who recognise the economics.

The operational implication is forward planning. Informal sourcing allows restocking on demand. Formal import requires forecasting 60 to 90 days ahead and placing quarterly orders. This is a real operational change. It is also how every mature supply chain in every industry operates — because demand forecasting is more cost-efficient than reactive restocking at a premium.

## The BTRC Dimension

Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulatory Commission regulates import of telecommunications equipment and accessories, including phone displays and related components. Formal import with BTRC-compliant documentation — correct product classification, CE certification, RoHS certification — provides a defensible compliance posture. Informal import with no documentation does not.

BTRC enforcement actions have increased in frequency and have targeted electronics imports with counterfeit type-approval documentation. A distributor operating through formal channels with correct documentation is not exposed to this enforcement risk. A distributor operating through informal channels is exposed to it each time a parcel enters the country.

Regulatory risk is a cost that does not appear on invoices but materialises as seizures, fines, and operational disruption. It is not predictable in timing, but it is predictable in direction: it increases as Bangladesh's regulatory environment for electronics imports matures, and it falls hardest on informal channels that have no documentation to produce when asked.

The distributors who are transitioning to formal import now are not doing so under regulatory compulsion. They are doing so because the economics increasingly justify it, and because the regulatory environment is moving in a direction that will eventually compel it anyway. Moving earlier is cheaper than moving under enforcement pressure.
