# Why Bangladesh's Motorcycle Workshops Are Overpaying for Parts — And Who's Responsible

Bangladesh has more than four million registered motorcycles on the road. Every one of them will need replacement parts. The market to serve that demand runs into the hundreds of millions of dollars annually — and almost none of it moves through channels that offer price transparency, quality documentation, or any form of recourse when something goes wrong.

That situation is not an accident. It is the predictable outcome of a supply chain built on informality, where each layer of the chain adds cost while removing accountability.

## The Scale of the Aftermarket Opportunity

With 350,000 to 450,000 new motorcycle registrations per year, Bangladesh's installed base grows by roughly ten percent annually. More significantly, the average motorcycle in Bangladesh is three to five years old — which means a growing share of the fleet is entering the highest-intensity replacement parts window. Engines accumulate wear. Body panels get damaged in traffic. Brake components wear down. The aftermarket grows faster than new registrations alone would suggest.

Hero and Bajaj together account for 60 to 65 percent of Bangladesh's motorcycle market. Their flagship models — Hero Splendor Plus, Bajaj Discover, Bajaj Pulsar 150 — each have well-documented parts consumption patterns. Body panels, brake shoes, and fuel tanks are the highest-turnover replacement items. A distributor serving 50 to 100 workshops needs consistent availability of these SKUs across multiple models. That consistency is precisely what the current supply chain fails to deliver.

## How Parts Currently Reach Bangladesh's Workshops

The informal supply chain for motorcycle parts operates through four main channels, each with its own set of problems.

**Luggage trade** is the oldest channel. Individual traders carry parts by hand from India — typically through the Benapole-Dhaka-Kolkata corridor. The economics are constrained by what fits in bags. There is no customs documentation, no certificate of authenticity, and no warranty. Volumes are small and lot-by-lot consistency is zero. The same trader on the same route may bring genuine Ludhiana-manufactured brake shoes one trip and uncertified substitutes the next, and neither the trader nor the buyer can reliably tell the difference.

**Informal courier** is the newer channel. Suppliers in Ludhiana or Shenzhen ship small parcels via DHL or FedEx, typically declared as samples or gifts to minimise duty exposure. NBR's parcel assessment is discretionary and inconsistent. Quality documentation — certificates of analysis, material specs, manufacturer credentials — is absent. Shops receive whatever the supplier sent, with no audit trail.

**The Dhaka grey market at Keraniganj** is where much of the above supply aggregates. Keraniganj dealers buy from luggage traders, courier importers, and other informal sources, then resell in bulk. Every link in that chain has added a margin. By the time a part reaches a Dhaka workshop through Keraniganj, it has passed through three or four hands, each extracting profit, and none of them has verified the product against a technical specification. OEM-standard parts and uncertified parts are routinely mixed in the same lot without labelling.

**Formal import** — licensed importers operating with an Import Registration Certificate, L/C or TT payment, documented certificates of analysis — represents a small minority of the market. Most workshops have never had access to this channel.

## Why the Informal Route Costs More

The informal route appears cheaper. The unit price from a Keraniganj dealer is lower than a formal import landed cost. This comparison is incomplete, and the incompleteness is expensive.

Defect rates tell the real story. Informal supply chains — with no manufacturer certification, no incoming quality control, and no lot traceability — run defect rates of 10 to 15 percent on motorcycle parts. Certified B2B importers operating with ISO 9001 or IATF 16949 certified manufacturers achieve defect rates of 1 to 3 percent. If a workshop buys 100 brake shoes from a Keraniganj dealer and 12 of them are defective, those 12 shoes must be absorbed as pure loss. There is no supplier to call. There is no warranty to invoke.

Specification consistency is a second hidden cost. The same part number from the same Keraniganj dealer varies in quality from lot to lot because the dealer is aggregating from multiple informal sources with no specification control. A workshop that builds its reputation on quality installations cannot build that reputation on inconsistent input materials. Every time a part fails prematurely, the workshop absorbs the redo cost, the customer relationship damage, and the reputational risk.

The margin layer calculation matters too. A part that passes through luggage trader, informal courier, and Keraniganj aggregator carries three separate margins before reaching the workshop. A direct formal import relationship eliminates those intermediate margins. At volume — which distributors serving 50 or more workshops can aggregate — the formal channel is often cheaper per unit than the grey market, despite paying full documented duties.

## The Safety Dimension That Nobody Discusses

Motorcycle parts are not uniform in their safety criticality. Decorative body panels are one thing. Brake assemblies and fuel tanks are another.

A brake shoe with the wrong friction lining composition — either too hard or too soft relative to specification — does not brake the way the motorcycle was engineered to brake. A harder-than-specified friction compound wears the drum faster than normal. A softer compound wears the shoe out at half the expected mileage and may fade under sustained braking. A workshop that installs a brake shoe cannot test its friction coefficient at the point of installation. They are trusting the supplier's quality system — or, in the informal channel, trusting nobody's quality system at all.

Fuel tanks have similar risk profiles. Poor weld penetration at the tank seam creates a leak point. Thin-gauge steel below specified wall thickness deforms under impact loads it should absorb. An inferior internal lining corrodes and contaminates fuel. These failures do not announce themselves at the point of installation. They emerge in the field, weeks or months later, creating liability for the workshop that installed the part.

Workshops that purchase informally carry this liability without knowing it. The question is not whether a substandard part will eventually create a problem. It is whether that problem will be traced back to the installer — and increasingly, in a market where customers are better informed and more likely to return, it is.

## The Distributor Layer and What It Requires

The economics of formal import make most sense at the distributor level, not the individual workshop level. A distributor serving 50 to 100 workshops can aggregate demand across models — Hero Splendor Plus, Bajaj Discover, Bajaj Pulsar 150, TVS Apache RTR — into a container-scale order. At container scale, per-unit landed costs fall, SAFTA preferential duty applies to India-origin parts, and the distributor can absorb the overhead of import documentation while spreading it across a large unit volume.

What that distributor needs from a supplier: consistent SKU availability across active models, manufacturer documentation (COA per lot, ISO 9001 / IATF 16949 certification, BIS certification for Indian-origin parts), and a supplier who understands Bangladesh's duty structure well enough to optimise landed cost. These are not conditions that informal channels can meet by definition.

The workshops that will thrive as Bangladesh's motorcycle market matures are the ones whose supply chain can deliver consistency. The ones that compete on installation quality, not just installation speed. That supply chain starts with a formal import partner.
