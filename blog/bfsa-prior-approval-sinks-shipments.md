# BFSA Prior Approval: The Food Import Requirement That Sinks Shipments Already at Sea

Most compliance failures in import operations are recoverable. Wrong documentation can be re-filed. Duty disputes can be negotiated. Even a port-of-entry inspection hold has a defined end point. But there is one category of compliance failure in Bangladesh food imports that is effectively unrecoverable once it occurs: shipping a food product that requires Bangladesh Food Safety Authority (BFSA) prior import permission without obtaining that permission before the vessel departs origin.

By the time you discover this problem, the cargo is on the water. The vessel cannot be recalled. The product cannot be returned to the supplier without commercial loss. The port detention clock starts the moment the container is discharged at Chattogram, and it runs continuously — regardless of how long BFSA takes to process an application that should have been submitted weeks earlier. This is the compliance failure that turns a profitable import into a crisis.

## The Prior Approval Requirement: What It Is and Why It Exists

Bangladesh Food Safety Authority was established under the Food Safety Act 2013 and given regulatory authority over food import through the Food Import Regulations 2015. For certain food product categories, BFSA determined that port-of-entry inspection alone is insufficient to protect public health — the products must be pre-approved for import before they enter the Bangladesh food supply chain.

The prior approval requirement reflects a risk-based regulatory logic: some food categories carry sufficient food safety risk — through contamination potential, additive concerns, or origin-country food safety record — that they require BFSA review before the importation event, not during it.

The practical consequence of this regulatory logic is that the approval process must be initiated and completed before the purchase order is confirmed and the supplier is instructed to load. For a food importer who doesn't understand this sequencing, the first indication that prior approval was needed is the BFSA hold notice at Chattogram port.

## Which Categories Require Prior Approval

The categories requiring BFSA prior import permission are defined in the Food Import Regulations 2015 and have been updated through subsequent notifications. The following categories represent the highest-volume import segments affected:

**Processed food products:** Packaged processed foods — including ready-to-eat products, packaged snacks, and processed meat or fish products — require prior BFSA approval. The rationale is that processing may introduce food additives, preservatives, or flavoring agents that Bangladesh's food safety framework regulates specifically.

**Imported dairy:** Skimmed milk powder, full-cream milk powder, condensed milk, and other dairy imports require prior approval. Dairy carries elevated food safety risk related to microbiological contamination, adulteration, and cold-chain integrity — making prior approval a logical regulatory response.

**Edible oils:** Sunflower oil, soybean oil, and other edible oils — particularly when imported in bulk or in packaged consumer formats — fall under prior approval requirements. The concern is both quality (rancidity, adulteration) and food safety (contaminant levels).

**Food additives and flavoring agents:** Any ingredient imported for use in food manufacturing that qualifies as a food additive under Bangladesh's standards requires BFSA prior approval before import.

**Products with novel ingredients:** Foods containing ingredients not previously recognized in Bangladesh's approved food additive or ingredient framework are subject to prior approval by definition.

This is not an exhaustive list. The applicable category determination requires review of the current BFSA regulations and notifications — a process that must happen at the sourcing stage, not at the clearance stage.

## The Sequence of Events When Prior Approval Is Missing

The following sequence is not hypothetical. It is what happens when a food product requiring BFSA prior approval arrives at Chattogram without that approval in place.

**Day 1:** Container is discharged at Chattogram Port Authority (CPA). Port free time begins — typically 3-4 days.

**Day 2-3:** The importer's customs agent files the Bill of Entry in ASYCUDA World. The filing triggers a BFSA hold flag in the system.

**Day 4-5:** CPA free time expires. Demurrage charges begin.

**Day 5-7:** The importer contacts BFSA to initiate a post-arrival application. BFSA advises that prior approval is required and must follow the standard application process. A post-arrival application does not receive expedited treatment simply because the cargo is already at port. The standard processing timeline — 2-4 weeks minimum — applies.

**Day 7-35+:** BFSA application is in process. Port demurrage accumulates daily. The importer has no certainty about the outcome: BFSA may approve, may approve with conditions, or may order the consignment re-exported or destroyed if the product presents a food safety concern.

**End state:** A 20-foot container of edible oil worth USD 40,000 CIF has accumulated USD 1,500-3,000 in port charges. The importer's client — a food manufacturer who planned production around this raw material delivery — has been disrupted. The commercial relationship is damaged. And the outcome of the BFSA review is still uncertain.

## What the Prior Approval Process Actually Involves

BFSA's prior import approval process is not opaque — it has defined inputs and outputs. What it has is a minimum timeline that must be respected.

**Application submission:** The importer submits an application to BFSA that includes product specifications (composition, ingredients, nutritional information), origin country food safety inspection certificate from a recognized authority, manufacturer details and factory registration documentation, intended use declaration, and BFSA application fee payment.

**BFSA review:** BFSA reviews the submitted documentation against Bangladesh food safety standards. For straightforward applications with complete documentation from established origins, this review takes 2-4 weeks. For novel products, products from origins without bilateral food safety recognition, or applications with incomplete documentation, the timeline extends.

**Approval issuance:** BFSA issues an import permission valid for the specified consignment or for a defined period (some approvals are consignment-specific, others are product-level approvals valid for multiple imports over a period).

The minimum realistic timeline from application submission to approval issuance is 2-4 weeks. This must be accounted for in the supply chain planning schedule — before the purchase order is placed, not after.

## The Just-in-Time Inventory Problem

The BFSA prior approval requirement creates a structural problem for food manufacturers and distributors operating on just-in-time inventory models. In a JIT environment, raw material delivery timing is the constraint that determines production scheduling. A 3-4 week delay in clearing an edible oil or dairy import doesn't just cost port charges — it stops production.

The math is straightforward: a food manufacturer processing 500 metric tonnes of edible oil per month with a 2-week production inventory buffer has approximately 14 days of runway before a port hold becomes a production stoppage. For a manufacturer operating on tighter inventory — as most do under working capital pressure — the threshold is lower.

The only reliable solution to this structural problem is an importer who manages BFSA prior approval at the sourcing stage, consistently, as a standard operating procedure for every applicable product category. There is no workaround at the clearance stage. The timeline is the timeline.

## The BFSA-BSTI Interaction

As noted in discussions of BSTI compliance, both BFSA and BSTI requirements apply independently to food imports. A product can be BFSA-approved and still require BSTI conformity assessment. Both must be managed, on parallel timelines, initiated at the sourcing stage.

For edible oils: BFSA prior import approval covers food safety parameters. BSTI conformity assessment covers conformance to the Bangladesh Standard for that oil type. An edible oil import requires both.

For processed dairy: BFSA prior approval covers microbiological and food safety standards. BSTI conformity covers the applicable BDS standard for the dairy product type. An imported dairy product requires both.

Managing both requirements on a single supply chain requires an importer who tracks both timelines simultaneously, coordinates with both regulatory bodies, and builds the combined compliance schedule into the purchase order timeline. An importer who is managing one and not the other is operating at the boundary of compliance — and that boundary is exactly where port holds occur.

## What a Well-Managed BFSA Compliance Process Looks Like

For a procurement manager evaluating food import partners, the indicators of a well-managed BFSA compliance process are specific:

The importer identifies the applicable BFSA prior approval requirement at the product inquiry stage — before the purchase order is discussed. They initiate the BFSA application process before confirming the order with the supplier. They confirm approval issuance before authorizing the supplier to arrange vessel booking. The BFSA approval document is in hand before the vessel departs origin.

This sequence — identify, apply, confirm approval, then ship — is the only one that reliably prevents port holds. Any importer who is not operating in this sequence on food commodity imports is operating without the compliance infrastructure that food import requires.
