# Bangladesh to the Gulf: How KHI Ships Potatoes and Handicrafts to UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia

Bangladesh has two export categories with genuine Gulf demand, strong production surplus, and government incentive support. Potatoes from Munshiganj. Handicrafts — Jamdani, Nakshi Kantha, jute, bamboo — from producers across Dhaka, Rajshahi, and Khulna divisions. The gap between this supply and the Gulf market is not agricultural or cultural. It is logistical, documentary, and commercial. Bridging that gap is what a trade operator does.

## The Bangladesh–Gulf Export Corridor

Chittagong to Jebel Ali (UAE): 12–15 days sea transit. Chittagong to Dammam (Saudi Arabia): 16–20 days. Chittagong to Hamad Port (Qatar): 14–18 days.

The corridor is established and relatively efficient. Bangladesh exports ready-made garments through it in enormous volumes. The challenge for agricultural and handicraft exports is not the corridor's existence — it is the documentation, quality control, and logistics infrastructure required to use the corridor commercially for products that most garment exporters have never touched.

## The Potato Export Opportunity: What the Numbers Say

Bangladesh is a top-10 global potato producer: 10–11 million metric tons annually. Domestic consumption absorbs approximately 7–8 million MT. The surplus — 2–3 million MT — represents the export opportunity.

Munshiganj district is the country's premium export-grade production zone. The riverine loam soils of Munshiganj produce potatoes with consistent dry matter content, clean skins, and natural disease resistance. Three varieties dominate export-grade production:

- **Diamant:** Yellow flesh, waxy texture, preferred in Gulf markets for curry dishes. Holds its shape under cooking. The premium variety in GCC fresh produce markets.
- **Cardinal:** Red skin, all-purpose use. Strong visual appeal at retail level.
- **Granola:** Floury texture, preferred for frying. Strong match with South Asian diaspora cooking preferences.

The 460+ cold storage facilities across Munshiganj, Bogura, and Rangpur districts mean December harvest can be held and exported through the May window — six months of supply availability, not just a brief post-harvest rush.

The Gulf demand is real. UAE has 3.5 million South Asian residents. Saudi Arabia has 4 million. Qatar, Kuwait, Oman — combined, more than 10 million South Asians in the GCC who grew up eating Diamant and Cardinal potatoes. UAE MOCCAE fresh produce import data reflects this demand in South Asian origin potato imports.

## The Four Barriers to Commercial Export

Why aren't more Bangladeshi producers exporting commercially to the Gulf? The barriers are not agricultural. The potatoes exist, the quality is there, and the diaspora market wants them. The barriers are operational.

**Barrier 1: Phytosanitary documentation**

The Department of Agricultural Extension (DAE) issues phytosanitary certificates for potato exports. Each destination country has its own specific pest clearance requirements. UAE MOCCAE requires pest-free certification for 12 specific potato pests including Potato cyst nematode (PCN), Potato moth (Phthorimaea operculella), and Colorado potato beetle. Saudi SFDA has its own specific pest list. Qatar MOPH adds further requirements.

A generic phytosanitary certificate will not satisfy these destination-specific requirements. The certificate must be prepared per destination, which requires DAE field inspections of the source production, coordination with the relevant foreign embassy or trade authority for format requirements, and processing time of 2–4 weeks. Producers who have never done this before typically underestimate the lead time and miss their export window.

**Barrier 2: Grading and sorting**

Gulf markets require consistent 50–80mm diameter grade A specification. Manual sorting at farm level is inherently inconsistent — a 22 MT container load assembled from 50 small farmer lots without mechanical grading will have size variation that fails GCC inspection at the destination port. Rejection or mandatory regrading at destination at the importer's cost.

Mechanical grading at a certified packing facility — with documented lot tracking — is the standard requirement for commercial-scale export. Setting up this packing capability is beyond most individual producers.

**Barrier 3: Reefer container logistics**

Gulf-bound potatoes require refrigerated containers: 4–8°C for Diamant and Cardinal (varieties with higher water content), 6–10°C for Granola. Reefer container space at Chattogram to Jebel Ali books out during the December–April export window. Reliable reefer space requires carrier relationships and forward booking — typically 6–8 weeks before required loading date.

A single 20-foot reefer container holds 21–22 MT of potatoes. To fill one container commercially, an operator needs to aggregate production from 50–200 small Munshiganj farmers — coordinating quality inspection, sorting, and packing across dozens of supply points.

**Barrier 4: GCC import compliance**

UAE MOCCAE requires online pre-notification through the system before the vessel arrives at Jebel Ali. Saudi SFDA has specific pesticide MRL (Maximum Residue Level) requirements that are more stringent than Codex Alimentarius standards on some molecules. Qatar and Kuwait have their own import portal registration requirements.

GCC import compliance is not a one-time setup. Regulations are updated regularly, and an exporter who was compliant last season must re-verify requirements before the current season's export. Staying current requires active monitoring of four separate GCC regulatory systems.

## EPB Incentive: Real Money on the Table

The Export Promotion Bureau offers a 20% cash incentive on potato export FOB value. This is not a rebate or duty drawback — it is a direct cash incentive paid through Bangladesh Bank after export documentation is submitted and verified.

On a $200,000 FOB export shipment (approximately 10 containers of 21 MT each), the EPB cash incentive is $40,000. This is real commercial value that professional exporters capture and that informal or undocumented exporters leave on the table.

EPB registration is required. Export documentation must be complete and accurate — shipping bill, bill of lading, phytosanitary certificate, commercial invoice, packing list. An operator who handles documentation correctly recovers this incentive within 60–90 days of export.

## The Handicraft Export Corridor

Bangladesh's handicraft export to the Gulf operates on different logistics but faces similar aggregation challenges.

**The products with export demand:**

- **Jamdani:** UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2013. Woven in Rupganj, Narayanganj. Gulf South Asian diaspora and high-end retail buyers recognise Jamdani's UNESCO status. Price premium over generic Bengali textiles is significant.
- **Nakshi Kantha:** Embroidered quilts, traditionally made by women artisans in rural Bangladesh. Strong appeal in lifestyle retail.
- **Jute products:** Bags, home décor, packaging. European and Gulf demand is consistent and growing.
- **Bamboo products:** Storage, tableware, home items. Growing demand in sustainability-conscious Gulf retail.

**The logistics challenge:** Handicraft production is artisan-scale, geographically dispersed, and inconsistent in finishing quality and packaging. A Gulf retail buyer needs consistent product specifications, export-grade packaging (price tags, SKU codes, country-of-origin labelling in Arabic and English), and minimum order quantities that individual artisan cooperatives cannot supply alone.

Aggregation — sourcing from multiple cooperatives, quality-checking finished goods, standardising packaging — is the commercial function that makes handicraft export viable at scale.

**EPB incentive for handicrafts:** 15% cash incentive on FOB value for handicraft exports. EPB registration and full documentation compliance required.

## The Commercial Gap

Both potato and handicraft export to the Gulf require the same core capability: a Bangladesh-side operator who can aggregate dispersed supply, manage phytosanitary and export documentation, coordinate specialised logistics (reefer for potatoes, standard containers with proper packing for handicrafts), maintain GCC import compliance across four regulatory systems, and recover government export incentives through correct documentation.

This is not a capability that individual farmers or artisan cooperatives have. It is the role of a trade operator with established carrier relationships, DAE and EPB registration, and active relationships with Gulf-side distribution partners who understand GCC import requirements.

The corridor exists. The demand exists. The production exists. The government incentives are real. The gap is the operational infrastructure to connect them — and that is precisely what a specialised trade operator provides.
