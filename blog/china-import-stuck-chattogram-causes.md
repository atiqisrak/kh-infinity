# Why Your China Import Got Stuck at Chattogram — And How a Good Importer Prevents It

You placed the order. The vessel sailed. Your supplier sent the shipping documents. And then — silence. Your cargo is sitting at Chattogram Port and no one is giving you a clear answer about when it will move. This situation is not unusual. It is, in fact, the predictable outcome of a set of specific, preventable failures that occur long before the container reaches the port gate. Understanding what actually causes China-to-Bangladesh clearance delays reveals exactly why the importer you choose matters more than any other variable in your supply chain.

## Failure Mode 1: Wrong HS Classification

Every product imported into Bangladesh must be declared under a specific Harmonized System (HS) code. That code determines the duty rate, the applicable regulatory requirements, and — critically — the risk channel assigned by Bangladesh's automated customs risk system, Be-FiRS (Better Facilitation and Risk System).

Generalist importers routinely misclassify products, particularly in technically specific categories. Smartphone displays fall under HS 8524.91.00 — not the broader 8517 or 9013 categories that a non-specialist might apply. Tarpaulin classifies under HS 6306.12.00 when made from polyethylene or polypropylene strip — a different classification from woven fabrics or tents. Motorcycle fuel tanks and body panels fall under HS 8714.10.00, distinct from general vehicle parts.

A wrong HS code does three things simultaneously: it applies the wrong duty rate (potentially costing you more than you budgeted, or triggering a dispute if you've underpaid), it flags the shipment in Be-FiRS for manual review, and it creates a documentation discrepancy that customs officers will investigate. The Be-FiRS manual review adds 7-14 working days to your clearance timeline — and that's before any duty dispute is resolved. Correct classification, applied at the filing stage, prevents all of this.

## Failure Mode 2: Missing BSTI or BTRC Pre-Clearance

Two of Bangladesh's key regulatory bodies — BSTI and BTRC — have requirements that must be fulfilled before your cargo arrives at port. An importer who manages these reactively, at the port gate, is an importer who will cost you weeks.

BTRC (Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulatory Commission) requires type approval for telecom equipment before it can be cleared for import. This includes smartphone displays, Android display assemblies, audio accessories, and charging equipment. A container of Shenzhen electronics that arrives at Chattogram without valid BTRC type approval documentation does not clear customs while that documentation is arranged. It sits in the port, accumulating charges.

BSTI (Bangladesh Standards and Testing Institution) conformity assessment requirements apply to industrial textiles — including tarpaulin and fabric goods — and to a significant list of food product categories. Like BTRC, BSTI compliance must be in place at the filing stage. Arranging it after container arrival is not an option under current Bangladesh customs procedures — it means your container waits.

A competent importer identifies the applicable BSTI and BTRC requirements at the purchase order stage, arranges the necessary documentation through recognized Conformity Assessment Bodies, and files with full compliance documentation ready. This is not complex for an operator who has done it before. It is an insurmountable delay for one who hasn't.

## Failure Mode 3: BFSA Food Safety Hold

For food product imports, Bangladesh Food Safety Authority (BFSA) oversight applies from the moment of import filing. Certain food categories — edible oils, dairy products, processed foods, food additives — require BFSA prior import permission before the vessel departs origin. Others are subject to BFSA inspection at port of entry.

A shipment that arrives at Chattogram without the required BFSA prior permission documentation is immediately placed on hold for BFSA inspection. BFSA inspection timelines are not guaranteed. While the inspection is pending — which can mean 10-21 additional days — port detention charges accumulate from day one.

The upstream prevention is straightforward: BFSA prior approval must be initiated at the purchase order stage, not after the shipment has been confirmed. For food commodity imports including sunflower oil, skimmed milk powder, soy sauce, sugar, and similar categories, the approval process requires product specifications, origin country inspection certificates, and BFSA application processing time. An importer who treats this as a post-arrival problem is structurally incapable of moving food cargo on time.

## Failure Mode 4: Documentation Mismatch

Chattogram customs operates on strict documentary consistency. The commercial invoice, packing list, and Letter of Credit must match each other — product description, quantity, unit of measure, country of origin, and value — with no discrepancies. A $1.00 value difference between the commercial invoice and the L/C is sufficient to trigger a manual assessment. A product described as "motorcycle spare parts" in the L/C but "fuel tank assembly" on the packing list is a discrepancy that invites officer scrutiny.

In practice, these mismatches occur because importers don't review supplier-generated documentation carefully before filing, or because they fail to instruct Chinese suppliers on exactly how Bangladesh customs documentation must be formatted. Chinese export documentation formats do not automatically align with Bangladesh customs requirements.

The prevention requires document review at origin — before the vessel sails. An importer who checks invoice, packing list, and L/C alignment before cargo is loaded is an importer who avoids the manual assessment queue. One who files whatever the supplier sends is one who discovers the discrepancy when the container is already sitting at the port.

## Failure Mode 5: Port Demurrage Escalation

Chattogram Port Authority (CPA) charges demurrage on containers that exceed the free time allowance in the container yard — typically 3-4 days for import containers. After free time expires, daily demurrage charges apply. For a standard 20-foot container, port detention costs can reach USD 500-2,000 over a 7-day delay, depending on the shipping line's applicable tariff and CPA yard charges. A 14-day delay doubles that.

These costs are not the result of customs complexity alone. They are also the result of slow filing. A CHA who has a backlog of other clients' filings, or who operates on a batch filing schedule rather than filing immediately on container arrival, is burning your free time allowance. Every day between container arrival and ASYCUDA World filing is a day of free time consumed before the customs process even begins.

An importer with an in-house customs team files immediately on container arrival notification — or in some cases, files advance documents before arrival using the ASYCUDA pre-arrival processing facility. This is the difference between minimizing demurrage exposure and discovering a significant unexpected cost buried in the port bill.

## Failure Mode 6: Bangladesh Bank and AD Bank Process Issues

The payment side of an import transaction has its own compliance sequence that must run in parallel with the logistics sequence. Letter of Credit issuance requires Authorized Dealer (AD) bank approval. Foreign exchange allocation must be secured within Bangladesh Bank's remittance framework. Utilization Declaration (UD) filing must occur in sequence. BRPD circular requirements apply to the documentation.

A missed step or a delay in the banking sequence does not just affect payment — it can freeze cargo release. Customs clearance and cargo release are linked to payment confirmation for L/C-based shipments. An importer who is not actively managing the AD bank process in parallel with the customs process creates a situation where the cargo is customs-cleared but cannot be released because the banking documentation is incomplete.

For China-to-Bangladesh imports — a corridor where the majority of transactions are L/C-based — this is a structural risk that must be managed proactively, not reactively.

Each of these six failure modes is preventable. None of them are acts of God or structural features of Bangladesh's import system. They are the predictable outcome of working with an importer who lacks the systems, the regulatory knowledge, or the in-house capacity to manage a China-to-Bangladesh shipment competently from order placement to cargo release.
