# Edible Oil Import to Bangladesh: Why Prices Vary 30% Between Importers for the Same Product

Two importers quote you sunflower oil. One says $800 per metric ton. The other says $1,150. Both call it sunflower oil. Both claim it meets BSTI specifications. Neither price is fabricated — but neither quote is for the same product. Understanding the difference between them is the difference between buying food-grade oil for your production line and discovering a compliance problem at Chattogram port.

## The Quote Comparison Problem Bangladesh's Oil Buyers Face Every Season

Bangladesh's edible oil market runs approximately 2 to 2.5 million metric tons per year. At that volume, even small per-unit price differentials have significant impact on a food manufacturer's input cost structure. The pressure on procurement teams to source cheaper is real and rational.

The problem is that comparing two edible oil quotes without a common specification framework is not a price comparison. It is a specification comparison masquerading as a price comparison. The gap between the cheapest and most expensive quote for what appears to be the same product routinely runs 20 to 40 percent — and every percentage point of that gap reflects a real variable, not arbitrary margin.

Five variables drive the gap. Understanding them transforms an opaque pricing situation into a manageable procurement decision.

## Variable 1 — Origin: Not All "Sunflower Oil" Has the Same Risk Profile

Origin is the first and most consequential variable in an edible oil quote comparison.

**Ukraine-origin sunflower oil** is the international benchmark. It is what global traders mean when they reference standard high-oleic sunflower oil specifications: 230 degrees Celsius smoke point, 41mg Vitamin E per 100g, consistent fatty acid profile. Ukraine is the world's largest sunflower oil exporter — or was, until the 2022 conflict disrupted export logistics from Black Sea ports. Ukraine-origin oil commands a premium and carries supply-disruption history that smart buyers have adjusted their sourcing strategies around.

**Argentina-origin sunflower oil** is the quality alternative. High-oleic Argentine sunflower varieties offer longer shelf life than conventional sunflower oil — a meaningful advantage in Bangladesh's humidity and temperature environment. Argentine export logistics are stable, and Argentina has become a more significant player in global sunflower oil trade precisely because buyers diversified away from single-source Ukraine dependence after 2022. Argentine-origin oil is typically priced at a moderate discount to Ukrainian, with comparable specification profiles.

**Russian-origin sunflower oil** is where the significant price discount appears — and where the compliance risk materialises. Russian-origin oil is available at meaningful cost reduction relative to Ukrainian origin. An importer quoting Russian-origin oil cheaply is passing the cost savings to you while potentially creating a bank-level compliance problem you did not anticipate. Many Bangladeshi banks operating under US correspondent banking relationships apply sanctions compliance review to payments involving Russian-origin commodity trade. An L/C for Russian-origin edible oil may face compliance review delays or refusal at the payment stage. The importer's bargain becomes your bank's problem.

**Malaysian and Indonesian palm oil** is a different product entirely. Palm oil and sunflower oil have different applications, different nutritional profiles, and different regulatory treatment under BSTI labelling requirements. Some unscrupulous sellers market partially hydrogenated palm blends with "sunflower" prominent in the product description. BFSA port sampling and BSTI-accredited laboratory testing catches this — but only after the goods have arrived, clearance has been attempted, and you have a shipment under hold rather than in production.

## Variable 2 — Product Specification: The Crude vs. RBD Distinction

Edible oil is not a single product. The refining stage determines what the product actually is.

RBD — Refined, Bleached, and Deodorised — sunflower or soyabean oil is consumer-ready and BSTI label-compliant for direct food manufacturing use. RBD is what a food manufacturer or retail packager requires.

Crude sunflower oil or crude soyabean oil is an input to the refining process. It is less expensive than RBD — sometimes significantly so — because it requires refining before it can be used in food production. If your facility does not have refining capacity, crude oil is not a viable purchase regardless of price.

Free Fatty Acid content is the specification variable that predicts shelf life and quality stability. FFA content below 0.1% is the benchmark for premium-grade RBD oil. Higher FFA content — sometimes sold as compliant because it falls within a broad BSTI tolerance — predicts faster oxidation, shorter shelf life, and potential taste impact in finished products. A quote on high-FFA oil is a cheaper product in a way that degrades your finished goods, not just your raw material.

## Variable 3 — Incoterms: The Headline Price vs. the Real Comparison Number

The most frequent source of price-comparison confusion in Bangladesh's edible oil import market is inconsistent Incoterms between competing quotes.

CIF Chattogram — Cost, Insurance, Freight to Chattogram — is the real comparison number. It includes the product cost, ocean freight to Bangladesh, and marine cargo insurance. When an importer quotes CIF, the number reflects the actual cost of getting the oil to the Bangladeshi port.

FOB origin — Free on Board at the export port — is a cheaper headline number because the freight and insurance costs are excluded. A $700 per metric ton FOB price for Ukrainian sunflower oil becomes approximately $850 to $900 per metric ton CIF Chattogram once ocean freight (which runs $80 to $120 per metric ton on the Ukraine-to-Bangladesh lane depending on vessel size and market conditions) and insurance are added.

Buyers who compare a FOB quote to a CIF quote are not comparing prices. They are comparing apples to a number that omits a meaningful cost component. An importer who quotes FOB and allows the buyer to assume it is CIF is either being imprecise or deliberately obscuring the comparison.

## Variable 4 — Duty Basis: The Most Consistently Under-Reported Cost

Total Tax Incidence on edible oil imports in Bangladesh is the variable most systematically absent from informal importer quotes, and the one with the largest absolute value.

For sunflower oil under the relevant HS code, the duty stack includes Customs Duty, Regulatory Duty, VAT applied on a cumulative base (CIF plus all duties to that point), and Advance Income Tax. The combined TTI on edible oils varies by product and applicable SRO classification — for sunflower oil, it can add 25 to 35 percent to the CIF value.

A $900 per metric ton CIF quote for sunflower oil becomes approximately $1,125 to $1,215 per metric ton after duties are applied. If an importer quotes $900 and describes the duties as something to be calculated later, the buyer's financial model for the purchase is built on an incomplete number.

Full landed cost — CIF Chattogram plus all applicable duties (Customs Duty, Regulatory Duty, VAT, AIT), plus Chattogram port charges, plus inland delivery to the buyer's facility — is the only number that enables a genuine procurement decision. Very few importers provide this as the default quote format. Buyers who do not ask for it are comparing partial numbers and making purchasing decisions on incomplete data.

## Variable 5 — Certification and Compliance Management: The Hidden Service Component

Two importers may quote the same CIF price and the same landed-cost estimate — and still represent different values because of what the quote includes in terms of compliance management.

BFSA prior import permit must be obtained before the vessel departs the origin port. The process requires product documentation submission and BFSA review. Some importers manage this as part of their standard service; others pass the process and its timeline uncertainty to the buyer. An importer who has not begun the BFSA prior permit process before signing a purchase order creates a vessel-departure delay that falls on the buyer.

BSTI label conformity is required for retail-packaged edible oil sold in Bangladesh. Converting bulk oil to retail-labelled packaging involves labelling compliance costs that vary by product and presentation format. Some importers include this in their quoted service; others present it as a separate cost after the purchase order is placed.

Halal certification from an IFB-recognised body is expected by institutional food manufacturers regardless of its technical regulatory status for vegetable-origin oil. An importer who cannot provide this documentation limits the buyer's own institutional supply chain documentation completeness.

## What a Complete Quote Looks Like

A procurement-ready edible oil quote contains: CIF Chattogram (specifying exact Incoterm and port), origin (country and region, not just country), product specification (RBD or crude, FFA content maximum, relevant quality parameters), all applicable duties calculated on the NBR's current SRO rates, Chattogram port handling and storage estimate, inland delivery cost to the buyer's facility, BFSA prior permit management confirmation, Halal certificate availability from IFB-recognised body, and the resulting total landed cost per metric ton.

That is one number. A clear, complete, auditable number that the buyer's procurement team can use for budgeting, planning, and comparison. The gap between importers who quote this way and those who don't is not a gap in price — it is a gap in what the buyer actually knows when they sign the purchase order.
