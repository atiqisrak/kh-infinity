# Counterfeit Motorcycle Parts in Bangladesh: How They Enter, Who Gets Hurt, and What Certified Sourcing Looks Like

BSTI enforcement actions targeting substandard motorcycle parts are not rare events. They are periodic confirmations of what anyone operating in Bangladesh's parts distribution market already knows: a significant share of the parts moving through informal channels are not what their labels claim. The question for distributors and workshop owners is not whether counterfeits exist in the market. It is whether their supply chain has the documentation to prove they are not buying them.

## How Counterfeits Enter the Market

The entry mechanisms for counterfeit motorcycle parts into Bangladesh's supply chain are not exotic. They exploit the absence of documentation requirements at each stage of an informal import chain.

**Origin mislabeling** is the most straightforward mechanism. Parts manufactured by uncertified facilities in India or China — factories with no ISO or IATF certification, no process control, and no quality management system — are packaged with labels reading "Original" or "Genuine" and in some cases with packaging that mimics branded OEM presentation. In the absence of testing equipment and manufacturer documentation, a distributor or workshop receiving these parts has no means to distinguish them from certified product. The label is the only evidence, and the label costs nothing to print.

**Specification mixing** operates at the aggregator level. Dhaka's grey market dealers — primarily in Keraniganj — source from multiple informal supply streams simultaneously. A single batch arriving at a dealer's warehouse may contain parts from three or four different manufacturers, some certified and some not, all mixed under the same part number. The dealer does not test and does not segregate. They sell the batch as uniform product. A workshop buying 50 brake shoes from such a dealer may receive 35 genuine parts and 15 uncertified ones, with no visual way to distinguish between them. The 15 uncertified parts go into customers' motorcycles.

**Grey market re-export** uses HS code ambiguity. Parts exported from India or China as "automobile components" under generic HS codes avoid brand-specific scrutiny at Bangladesh customs. The documentation describes the product accurately enough to clear customs without triggering examination, while providing no information about manufacturer identity, certification status, or specification compliance. Customs clearance is not quality certification. A part that clears Chattogram port is not a part that meets specification.

**Informal import channels** are structurally incapable of filtering counterfeits because they have no mechanism to do so. Luggage traders compete on price. Courier importers compete on speed. Keraniganj aggregators compete on availability. None of these competitive pressures select for quality. All of them select against documentation overhead, which is the only mechanism that distinguishes certified from uncertified product.

## What Counterfeits Actually Look Like in the Field

Abstract descriptions of quality failure are less useful than specific, observable characteristics. Each major product category has its own failure signatures.

**Fuel tanks** manufactured below specification are identifiable to an experienced eye, though not always at point of purchase. Steel gauge below specified wall thickness produces a tank that flexes under hand pressure where a genuine tank does not. Weld penetration at the tank seam is visible under direct light — insufficient penetration leaves a visible gap or undercut at the weld toe that proper fusion does not. The internal lining matters for longevity: genuine-specification tanks use an epoxy internal coating that prevents corrosion and fuel contamination; uncertified tanks may use inferior coatings that begin to delaminate within a riding season, contaminating the fuel system. External coating quality on counterfeit tanks is typically lower — powder coat applied over inadequate surface preparation, leading to adhesion failure and corrosion within months in Bangladesh's humid climate.

**Body panels** tell the story through UV stability and dimensional accuracy. ABS plastic formulations vary in UV stabiliser content. Genuine-specification panels for Bangladesh's market use UV-stabilised ABS that resists discolouration under tropical sun exposure. Counterfeit panels use lower-cost ABS without UV stabiliser — they bleach white and become brittle within a single dry season. Dimensional accuracy is equally telling: genuine panels are moulded to specification, with mounting holes positioned to fit the motorcycle's frame geometry without modification. Counterfeit panels — produced on copied moulds of varying quality — routinely have mounting holes misaligned by two to four millimetres, requiring a mechanic to drill out or elongate holes for installation. That misalignment is a daily frustration for the installer and a visible sign of quality to the customer watching the installation.

**Brake shoes** carry the highest safety stakes and the most consequential quality variation. The friction lining compound — the material that contacts the drum to generate braking force — must match a specified friction coefficient range for the vehicle design. A harder-than-specification compound generates sufficient braking force but transmits excessive heat and abrasion to the drum, wearing the drum surface faster than normal. A softer-than-specification compound provides reduced braking force and wears out in half or less of the specified service life. Neither failure is immediately obvious at installation. Both create problems in the field weeks or months later. Additionally, counterfeit brake components frequently omit asbestos-free certification — asbestos as a friction liner is prohibited in most responsible manufacturing, but its presence in uncertified product cannot be assumed absent.

## What the IATF 16949 Standard Actually Requires

The International Automotive Task Force standard — IATF 16949 — is the quality management framework for automotive parts manufacturing. It is worth understanding what certification actually means at the factory level, because the standard is frequently cited as a credential without explanation of its substance.

IATF 16949 requires a complete quality management system at the process level, not just at the final inspection stage. Certified manufacturers must maintain control plans for every production process — documenting the process parameters, measurement methods, reaction plans for out-of-control conditions, and acceptance criteria at each step. They must conduct measurement system analysis to confirm that their testing equipment is capable of detecting the variation they need to detect. They must maintain records of statistical process control where applicable, and they must have documented corrective action processes for nonconforming product.

Critically, an IATF 16949 certified factory cannot ship off-specification product without triggering internal alert systems. When a process goes out of control, the control plan specifies what happens — production stops, the nonconforming product is segregated, root cause analysis is initiated. This is the structural difference between a certified manufacturer and an uncertified one: the certified manufacturer has systems that make quality failures visible internally, before shipment. The uncertified manufacturer has no such systems.

## The Documentation That Distinguishes Certified Supply

Documentation is the only reliable mechanism for distinguishing certified from uncertified product in a market where counterfeits are visually indistinguishable from genuine parts. Any distributor or workshop owner sourcing at volume should be able to obtain and verify the following from their supplier:

**ISO 9001 / IATF 16949 manufacturer certificate**: issued by an accredited certification body (Bureau Veritas, TÜV, Intertek, SGS are common), with the manufacturer's name, scope, and certificate validity dates. The certificate should be verifiable against the certification body's registry.

**BIS certification** for Indian-origin parts: the Bureau of Indian Standards issues BIS certification for automotive components sold into the Indian market. Parts exported from India's certified manufacturing cluster carry this certification as evidence of conformity to Indian national standards.

**Certificate of Analysis per lot**: a COA specifies the material properties and dimensional characteristics of a specific production lot, with measured values against specified limits. A COA is lot-specific — it is not a generic document but a record tied to the specific shipment.

**Asbestos-free declaration** for brake components: a signed declaration from the manufacturer confirming that no asbestos-containing materials are used in friction linings. Required for responsible procurement of brake components.

**Packaging integrity and part number traceability**: genuine certified product carries part numbers that trace back to the manufacturer's documentation system. Lot codes on packaging should correspond to COA records.

If a supplier cannot produce these documents, the product's provenance cannot be verified. The absence of documentation is itself information.
