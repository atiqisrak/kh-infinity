# Bangladesh Motorcycle Market 2026: 400,000 New Registrations and the Parts Import Boom No One Talks About

The headline number in Bangladesh's motorcycle market is always new registrations — 350,000, 400,000, 450,000 units annually, depending on the year. That number gets attention from OEMs, assemblers, and consumer media. The number that doesn't get the same attention is the installed base: 4 million registered motorcycles, accumulating years of use, requiring parts.

The aftermarket parts market is structurally larger than the new vehicle market in terms of ongoing commercial opportunity, and it is growing faster — because every motorcycle registered today becomes a parts customer for the next 7–10 years. The formal B2B import channel for aftermarket parts is the only supply chain positioned to serve this at scale.

## The Fleet Fundamentals

Bangladesh's BRTA (Bangladesh Road Transport Authority) registration data shows an installed base that has grown from under 1 million motorcycles in 2010 to over 4 million by 2024. The growth drivers are structural, not cyclical:

**Last-mile delivery economy:** Food delivery (Shohoz, Foodpanda), parcel delivery (Pathao, Paperfly, eCourier), and ride-hailing (Shohoz, Pathao) have created hundreds of thousands of professional motorcycle riders in Dhaka, Chattogram, Sylhet, and secondary cities. These delivery riders depend on their motorcycles for income. Their bikes run 60–80 km per day under continuous use — far above the 30–40 km/day of a typical private commuter.

**Rural mobility:** In districts where public bus routes are infrequent and roads are narrow, motorcycles are the primary personal transport mode. Bangladesh's rural road network improvements over the last decade have expanded motorcycle penetration into areas that previously relied on cycle rickshaws.

**Affordability:** Entry-level 100–125cc motorcycles from Hero, Bajaj, and Walton start at BDT 80,000–120,000 ($725–$1,090). At current Bangladeshi income levels, this is an achievable purchase — particularly with dealer financing. The motorcycle is not a luxury purchase; it is a productive asset.

New registrations of 350,000–450,000 annually compound onto a growing installed base. The fleet grows by more than 10% per year.

## Brand Market Share: Why Hero and Bajaj Define the Parts Market

Understanding the aftermarket parts opportunity requires understanding who makes up the installed base.

**Hero MotoCorp (India): approximately 40% market share.** Hero is Bangladesh's dominant motorcycle brand, led by the Hero Splendor Plus — the best-selling motorcycle in Bangladesh by volume, and probably the most recognisable motorcycle in the country. The Splendor Plus has dominated the 100cc commuter segment for over a decade. Hero Glamour and Hero HF Deluxe complete the core Hero lineup in Bangladesh.

**Bajaj Auto (India): approximately 22% market share.** Bajaj's portfolio spans commuter and sport segments. The Bajaj Discover 125 is the volume commuter. The Bajaj Pulsar 150 is the entry-level sport-commuter with the highest brand recognition in the 150cc class. The Bajaj Boxer is widely used by commercial riders — delivery fleets, rural commuters — for its durability and fuel economy.

**Honda (Japan, assembled locally): approximately 12% market share.** Honda competes in the premium commuter and mid-range segments. Honda Shine and Honda CB Unicorn are the primary models.

**TVS Motor (India): approximately 8% market share.** TVS Apache RTR 160 and TVS Metro have established segments in the sport-commuter and commuter categories.

**Yamaha: approximately 5%.** Premium positioning.

**Others (Walton and assembled Chinese brands): approximately 13%.**

Hero and Bajaj together hold 60–65% of Bangladesh's 4 million-unit fleet. That is 2.5–2.6 million Hero and Bajaj motorcycles in active use across the country. The parts ecosystem for these two brands IS the aftermarket market in any practical sense. Brake shoes, body panels, fuel tanks, engine components — for Hero Splendor, Bajaj Discover, and Bajaj Pulsar 150 — represent the overwhelming majority of replacement demand by volume and value.

## The Aftermarket Timing Cycle: Why Right Now Is the Peak

The 1.5 million motorcycles registered in Bangladesh between 2018 and 2020 are now 5–7 years old. Average usage intensity in Bangladesh — particularly for delivery riders — is 15,000–25,000 km per year. At this mileage accumulation:

**Brake shoes:** Replace every 12–18 months under normal use, faster under delivery-rider intensity. The 2018–2020 cohort has already seen 4–5 brake shoe replacement cycles. This is recurring demand, not one-time.

**Body panels:** Bangladesh's road accident rate is among South Asia's highest. Motorcycles are frequently involved in minor collisions at low speed that damage fairings, side panels, and fuel tank covers. Body panel replacement is driven by accident frequency and by the upgrade culture — riders who want their 5-year-old Splendor to look new.

**Fuel tanks:** Corrosion and accident damage drive fuel tank replacement. Motorcycles registered in 2018–2020 are now at the 5–8 year mark where corrosion from fuel chemistry becomes a replacement driver, particularly in coastal and high-humidity areas like Chattogram and coastal southern Bangladesh.

**Engine components:** Piston rings, gaskets, carburettors, CDI units. Long-use motorcycles in the 100,000 km range begin needing internal engine servicing. The 2018–2020 cohort is approaching this range.

The replacement cycle is compounding: new parts demand from recent registrations, plus accelerating replacement demand from the aging installed base, simultaneously. This is the structural basis for the parts import boom that the headline new-registration numbers do not capture.

## The Professionalization of the Aftermarket

The most important structural change in Bangladesh's motorcycle aftermarket is the professionalization of the workshop sector. And this change has direct implications for how parts are sourced.

Single-mechanic roadside shops — the informal curbside workshops that have served Bangladesh's motorcycle market for decades — are increasingly supplemented by and in some cases replaced by multi-bay service centres. These professional workshops operate in:

- **Dhaka:** Mirpur, Keraniganj, Demra (major workshop concentrations)
- **Chattogram:** Agrabad, Muradpur, Oxygen More
- **Sylhet, Rajshahi, Khulna:** Secondary city centres with established workshop districts

Professional multi-bay workshops have different procurement requirements than informal single-mechanic shops. They need:

**Consistent availability:** A professional workshop cannot turn away a customer waiting for a Hero Splendor brake shoe because their informal sourcing contact doesn't have stock this week. Reliable stock from a formal importer is a business requirement.

**Documentation:** Warranty claims, customer receipts, and increasingly, service records that fleet operators (delivery companies) require. This documentation requires parts sourcing from traceable channels.

**Quality assurance:** The grey-market counterfeit parts problem in Bangladesh's motorcycle aftermarket is well-documented (and explored in a separate KHI post). Professional workshops staking their reputation on quality service cannot risk fitting counterfeit parts that fail prematurely.

**Volume pricing:** Professional workshops fitting 30–50 brake shoes per week need distributor pricing, not retail pricing. This requires a formal B2B supply relationship.

The professionalization trend is not finished — Bangladesh's workshop sector is still in early stages compared to India's more mature market. But the direction is established, and the professional workshops that are scaling now are building supply relationships now.

## The Import Chain: Ludhiana to Chattogram

The premier source for Hero, Bajaj, TVS, and Honda-compatible aftermarket parts is Ludhiana, Punjab, India — India's motorcycle parts manufacturing hub. Ludhiana manufacturers supply the Indian domestic aftermarket and export to Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Southeast Asia.

Quality differentiation in Ludhiana production:

- **ISO 9001 / IATF 16949 certified manufacturers:** Factory quality systems that ensure dimensional accuracy, material specification compliance, and documented testing. Parts from these manufacturers perform reliably and consistently.
- **Mid-tier manufacturers:** Good quality, reasonable consistency, no certification. Acceptable for price-sensitive segments.
- **Low-tier manufacturers:** Variable quality, no certification, inconsistent raw material sourcing. The source of many counterfeit-risk parts in the grey market.

The importer who has direct relationships with specific Ludhiana manufacturers — with documented factory certifications and quality inspection — delivers a fundamentally different product than an importer sourcing through intermediary agents without factory visibility.

Bangladesh's import duty structure for motorcycle parts and the documentation requirements for BSTI-classified components (where applicable) mean that formal importers with proper documentation also provide compliant supply — allowing buyers to resell without compliance risk.

## The Market's Direction

Bangladesh's motorcycle market has a clear medium-term trajectory: continued registration growth (the underlying demand drivers — last-mile delivery, rural mobility, affordability — are structural, not cyclical), fleet aging, and workshop professionalization. All three vectors compound the aftermarket parts opportunity.

The formal B2B import channel — certified manufacturer relationships, proper documentation, reliable volume supply — is positioned to capture the growth that the informal grey-market channel cannot serve. Professional workshops will not build their business on unreliable, undocumented sourcing.

The parts import boom is not a future prediction. It is happening now, driven by the 4 million motorcycles already on Bangladesh's roads.
