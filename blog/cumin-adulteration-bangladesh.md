# Cumin Import to Bangladesh: Why Adulteration Is the Industry's Worst-Kept Secret

Cumin is Bangladesh's most widely used whole spice and one of its most consistently adulterated imports. The adulteration is not new, and it is not subtle — BFSA has documented enforcement actions, spice millers have received lots that tested below half the specified volatile oil content, and informal broker networks continue to move adulterated material because buyers either do not test or do not know what to test for.

The buyers who do not have this problem are not smarter. They are buying differently.

## The Four Forms of Cumin Adulteration Documented in Bangladesh's Market

Cumin (Jeera, classified under HS 0909.21.00 for whole seed and 0909.22.00 for ground) adulteration in Bangladesh's import market takes several forms, ranging from crudely detectable to analytically sophisticated.

**Filth addition** is the most basic form. Sand, small stones, dried grass stems, and soil are added to increase the weight of a lot — a direct reduction in product value that increases the seller's margin. This level of adulteration is visible under proper inspection and detectable by a trained buyer who knows what clean cumin should look like. The persistence of filth adulteration in spot-market lots reflects the fact that many buyers do not inspect, or accept inspection reports that are not independent.

**Low-quality seed mixing** is harder to detect visually but straightforward to catch analytically. Cumin from inferior origins — lower aromatic intensity, smaller seed size, different color profile — is mixed with premium Unjha-origin cumin in proportions that allow the blend to pass visual inspection. The blend may look acceptable under non-specialist examination. It will not pass volatile oil content testing, where the inferior-origin seed contribution pulls the blend below specification.

**Origin mislabeling** is systematic and commercial in scale. Iranian cumin and Turkish cumin — both legitimate products in their own markets, but lower in aromatic quality and different in appearance from Indian Unjha-origin — are sold as Indian Unjha origin at Unjha price premiums. The price differential between Unjha Grade A and Iranian origin can run 30 to 40 percent. An importer who buys Iranian cumin and declares it Unjha origin is capturing that differential at the buyer's expense.

**Fumigation certificate fraud** is the compliance-specific form of adulteration. Fumigation certificates are issued without effective treatment having been carried out, or are issued for treatments applied below effective dose rates. Bangladesh Food Safety Authority detects pest contamination — Trogoderma granarium (Khapra beetle) is the principal quarantine pest concern for cumin from the Indian subcontinent — through port sampling. A lot arriving with a fraudulent fumigation certificate and live pest contamination faces port hold, mandatory treatment, and potentially destruction. The buyer who arranged the shipment on the basis of a fraudulent certificate bears the commercial consequence.

## The Volatile Oil Standard: The One Number That Defines Cumin Quality

The single most important quality indicator for cumin is volatile oil content, measured as a percentage by steam distillation. Volatile oil is the fraction responsible for cumin's characteristic aromatic intensity — the compound that makes cumin taste and smell like cumin rather than a neutral ground spice.

The quality spectrum runs as follows:

**Premium Unjha Grade A**: 3.0 to 4.5 percent volatile oil. This is the specification that Bangladesh's institutional spice millers, food manufacturers, and premium spice brands require. Cumin at 3.0 percent volatile oil minimum delivers the aromatic intensity that consumers expect from products using the ingredient.

**Standard Indian-origin cumin**: 2.5 to 3.0 percent volatile oil. Acceptable for commodity applications where premium aromatic intensity is not required. Still genuinely Indian-origin, still reasonably clean, but below the quality profile of Unjha Grade A.

**Low-grade or adulterated blend**: below 2.5 percent volatile oil. At this level, the cumin is delivering materially less aromatic content per kilogram than the specification requires. The spice miller who uses this material produces ground cumin that tastes weaker than competitive products — consumer-detectable difference that accumulates into brand quality complaints.

**Severely adulterated material**: below 1.5 percent volatile oil. This is not cumin in any commercially meaningful sense. It is a cumin-flavored product made primarily from non-cumin material. BFSA laboratory testing catches this. But it has to be tested to be caught.

The arithmetic is stark: a spice miller who receives 50 metric tons of cumin at 1.8 percent volatile oil instead of the specified 3.0 percent has purchased a lot that delivers 40 percent less aromatic content per kilogram than they planned for. They have two options: use it and produce weaker product, or discard it and repurchase at full market price. Neither outcome was in the procurement plan.

## Unjha and Why Origin Verification Is Not a Branding Exercise

Unjha, Gujarat, India is the world's largest cumin trading hub. The Unjha APMC (Agricultural Produce Market Committee) mandi — the wholesale market where cumin is traded, graded, and priced — sets the global benchmark price for high-quality cumin. Cumin that can be verifiably attributed to Unjha-origin, traded through the APMC system, AGMARK graded, and third-party tested for volatile oil content is the gold standard for the Bangladesh institutional market.

The Unjha connection is not merely a quality claim. The APMC mandi system creates a paper trail — lot receipts, grade certificates, trader documentation — that can be followed from the mandi to the export point. Spices Board India certification at origin adds another layer of documented traceability. This documentation chain is what separates a genuine Unjha-origin lot from an Iranian or Turkish lot sold with a false origin declaration.

Obtaining this documentation requires working with exporters who participate in the formal APMC mandi system rather than buying through informal spot-market brokers who aggregate from multiple sources without origin traceability. Informal brokers compete on price, which is how they win spot-market business. They cannot compete on origin documentation, because they do not have it.

## The Testing Requirements That Make Adulteration Detectable — But Only If You Test

The documentation and testing package that definitively confirms cumin quality and origin includes several elements, each of which addresses a specific adulteration risk.

A **Certificate of Analysis** from a NABL-accredited or internationally recognised laboratory (SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek) showing volatile oil content by steam distillation is the single most important quality document. No COA with a volatile oil measurement means no confirmed specification compliance. This is not optional for institutional buyers. It is the baseline.

**Sortex cleaning certificate** documents that the lot has passed through an optical colour-sorting machine that removes foreign matter, discoloured seeds, stones, and other filth material. Sortex-cleaned cumin has materially lower filth content than non-Sortex material. The certificate confirms the cleaning was performed, not merely claimed.

**Origin certificate from Spices Board India** is the official government document confirming Indian origin and the specific origin state where required. Spices Board India is the statutory authority for Indian spice exports; their certificate is the strongest available origin documentation.

**Fumigation certificate from an approved fumigant provider** — specifying the active ingredient, dose rate, exposure duration, and date of treatment — is the BFSA compliance document for quarantine pest control. Approved fumigants in Bangladesh's regulatory context include methyl bromide (where permitted) and phosphine. The certificate must come from a recognised fumigation service provider, not a handwritten statement from the exporter.

**SGS or Bureau Veritas pre-shipment inspection report** provides independent third-party confirmation of quantity, quality, and specification compliance at the origin point before the goods are loaded. This is what informal brokers cannot provide, because pre-shipment inspection requires scheduling an independent inspector at the exporter's facility — a process that exposes non-conforming goods before shipment rather than after.

## The Question That Filters Out the Risk

There is one question that every cumin buyer should ask every supplier before placing a purchase order. It is not a trick question, and it requires no specialist knowledge to interpret the answer: "Can you provide a Certificate of Analysis from SGS, Bureau Veritas, or Intertek showing volatile oil content for this lot?"

If the answer is yes, and the certificate is produced promptly, the supplier has a testable quality system for their product. The COA number and the testing laboratory can be verified.

If the answer is a variant of "we guarantee quality" without producing the document, the supplier cannot confirm their own specification compliance through independent analysis. They may be right about their quality. They may not be. You cannot tell which, and you will discover which when your mill runs the lot and your customers notice the difference.

The informal broker network that moves adulterated cumin through Bangladesh's spice import channel competes on price. The differential between an Unjha Grade A lot with a full documentation package and a spot-market lot with no documentation is real — and it is smaller than the differential between a mill that produces consistent-quality ground cumin and one that produces batches that vary by 40 percent in aromatic intensity depending on which lot's material is running that week.
