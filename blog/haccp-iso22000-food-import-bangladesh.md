# Why Bangladesh's Top Food Manufacturers Now Demand HACCP and ISO 22000 from Their Importers — Not Just Low Prices

Procurement teams at Bangladesh's largest food manufacturers used to ask one question: "What's the price?" Today, they ask a second question first — one that most importers still can't answer properly: "What's your food safety certification?"

## The Procurement Standards Shift That Happened Without a Press Release

Bangladesh's leading food manufacturers — PRAN (one of the largest FMCG groups in the country), ACI Limited, Meghna Group, Danish Foods, Bombay Sweets — have systematically upgraded their supplier qualification requirements over the past three to five years. The shift was gradual, driven by forces well above their procurement teams' pay grade, and it went largely unannounced in trade publications. But its effects are now visible at every level of Bangladesh's food import chain.

Historically, these companies required food safety documentation from their overseas manufacturers. A Certificate of Analysis from the factory, a HACCP plan from the production facility, an ISO 22000 audit from the processing plant — these were manufacturer-level requirements. The importer in between was evaluated primarily on price, delivery speed, and customs clearance efficiency.

That model no longer holds for Bangladesh's institutional-grade buyers. They now require food safety documentation from their importer intermediaries too. Not instead of manufacturer documentation — in addition to it.

## Why the Shift Happened: Three Converging Pressures

**Export market requirements created a documentation cascade.** Companies like PRAN and ACI export processed foods to the USA, UK, Middle East, and India. Their international buyers require full supply-chain traceability — not just from the factory gate but from every node in the chain, including the importer tier. When a UK supermarket buyer asks PRAN to demonstrate that the sunflower oil in their biscuit line came from an ISO 22000-compliant supply chain, PRAN cannot satisfy that requirement if their Bangladeshi importer maintains no food safety management system. HACCP certification at every supply chain node is not a preference for many export market buyers — it is a contract requirement.

This pressure cascades backward. Exporters like PRAN pull their own procurement documentation standards up to meet export-market requirements. Their importers either meet those standards or lose the account.

**BFSA enforcement escalation changed the economics of clearance.** The Bangladesh Food Safety Authority has increased port inspection frequency and scope over the same period. The change in inspection approach is meaningful: lots arriving with HACCP-documented importer records — showing hazard analysis for the product category, documented storage and handling controls, and traceability to origin — receive preferential processing flow at Chattogram. Lots without importer-level food safety documentation enter a longer queue that can add days to clearance timelines.

For institutional buyers managing production schedules tied to raw material arrivals, a three-day delay in edible oil clearance is not a minor administrative inconvenience. It is a production line disruption. Importers who carry HACCP documentation are, in effect, reducing the operational risk of buying from them.

**Corporate reputational risk reshaped procurement logic.** Bangladesh has experienced high-profile food safety incidents — contaminated cooking oil, substandard dairy products, adulterated spices — that resulted in significant brand damage for companies whose supply chains failed. Large FMCG companies now manage supply-chain risk through documentation for one clear reason: if a contamination incident occurs, they need a paper trail that demonstrates due diligence at every supply chain stage. An importer-level HACCP record shifts accountability to the right party — the manufacturer or importer who introduced the hazard — rather than leaving a gap in the institutional buyer's own procurement documentation.

## What HACCP Actually Means in an Import Context

HACCP — Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points — was designed for food processing environments, but the framework applies directly to the import supply chain. For an importer operating in Bangladesh's commodity food sector, this translates as follows:

**Hazard identification** covers the food safety risks that could enter the supply chain at four stages: sourcing (origin quality failures, contamination at production), transit (temperature excursions, moisture exposure, pest ingress in shipping containers), port handling and storage (Chattogram's humidity and temperature environment creates specific risks for moisture-sensitive commodities like milk powder and almonds), and final delivery to the buyer's facility.

**Critical Control Points** for a typical importer are specific and documentable. For a milk powder importer: moisture testing on arrival at warehouse (humidity damage is the principal risk for SMP in Bangladesh's climate), temperature monitoring during Chattogram port storage, and pest control documentation at the storage facility. For a cumin importer: volatile oil testing on arrival to verify specification compliance, foreign matter inspection at goods receipt, and fumigation status verification before storage.

**Monitoring and corrective action** means documented records — what was checked, when it was checked, who conducted the check, what the result was, and what action was taken when a result fell outside specification. This is not a theoretical exercise. HACCP's value to a buyer is the audit trail: if a quality problem emerges at their production facility, they can trace it backward through the importer's records to the origin lot.

## ISO 22000: The Standard That Institutional Buyers Now Recognize

ISO 22000 is a Food Safety Management System standard — more comprehensive than a standalone HACCP plan in that it incorporates HACCP within a broader management system framework that includes food safety policy, management review, internal audit, and continual improvement processes.

The distinction matters because ISO 22000 is internationally recognized and audit-verified by third-party certification bodies. An importer that holds ISO 22000 certification has been assessed by an independent auditor against an internationally recognized standard — not just internally documented a hazard analysis. For procurement teams at PRAN or Meghna Group, ISO 22000 certification from a recognized body provides assurance that doesn't require them to conduct their own audit of every supplier.

This is increasingly what institutional buyers expect: a certified food safety management system at the importer level, not just a promise that the manufacturer's documentation is in order.

## The Practical Benefit for Institutional Buyers

The value of buying from an importer with HACCP and ISO 22000-aligned practices is not abstract. It shows up in three measurable places:

Fewer BFSA holds and faster clearance, because importer-level documentation reduces the inspection burden at port. Faster clearance means more predictable raw material arrivals and fewer production schedule disruptions.

Supply-chain documentation that satisfies institutional buyers' own quality teams. When a PRAN quality manager asks their procurement team for importer documentation, the answer should not require a week of email exchanges. An importer with a documented food safety management system provides certificates, COAs, and inspection records as standard components of the shipment documentation package.

Risk transfer to the appropriate party. If a quality failure occurs in an imported commodity, the buyer who purchased from a documented, certified importer has the paper trail to demonstrate that their procurement process was sound. The importer's documentation is part of the buyer's own risk management.

## What to Look for When Evaluating an Importer's Food Safety Credentials

The right questions to ask any importer competing for institutional food manufacturer business: Can you provide evidence of HACCP implementation across your import handling process — not just at manufacturer level? Do you maintain a product-specific Certificate of Analysis for each lot? What is your corrective action procedure when a lot arrives outside specification? Can you provide storage facility documentation demonstrating temperature, humidity, and pest control controls?

If the answers are vague or the documentation is slow to arrive, the importer's food safety system exists on paper, not in practice.
