Every import into Bangladesh that involves foreign currency payment is governed by Bangladesh Bank's Foreign Exchange Regulations. The Central Bank controls the outflow of foreign exchange — dollars, euros, renminbi, rupees — through a combination of regulations, circulars, and periodic updates that affect how importers pay their overseas suppliers.

For importers who treat this as a back-office function — handled by the bank, not their concern — the regulations create intermittent surprises: payments delayed, Letters of Credit refused, shipments funded differently than planned. For importers who understand the framework, these are predictable and manageable constraints.

## The Core Payment Mechanisms

**Letters of Credit (LC):** The dominant payment instrument for commercial imports into Bangladesh. An LC is a guarantee issued by a Bangladesh-based bank (the issuing bank) on behalf of the importer to a foreign supplier, promising payment against specific documents. Bangladesh Bank regulates LC issuance — the authorized limits banks can issue, the tenors allowed, the acceptable documents, and the foreign currency accounts that settle payment.

**Advance Payment (TT / Wire Transfer):** For imports below certain thresholds, importers can remit payment directly to suppliers via telegraphic transfer without an LC. Bangladesh Bank sets the per-consignment and per-year limits for advance payment remittances. Importers who source small quantities or sample shipments frequently use this mechanism. The limits and procedural requirements for advance payment have been revised multiple times in 2024-25, and some importers who relied on informal understanding of the rules have encountered blocked transfers.

**Deferred Payment / Usance LC:** An LC structure where the supplier ships against a deferred payment obligation — typically 90, 180, or 360 days. The importer receives the goods but settles the LC later. Bangladesh Bank restricts deferred payment tenors for certain product categories and has periodically tightened the conditions under which usance LCs can be opened by banks.

## What Changed in 2024-25

Bangladesh faced significant foreign exchange reserve pressure in 2023-2024. In response, Bangladesh Bank issued a series of FE circulars and policy letters that modified import payment conditions in ways that directly affected importers:

**LC margin requirements increased.** Banks are required to collect a cash margin from importers when issuing LCs — the percentage held in reserve against the LC value. During the reserve pressure period, banks increased required margins from the typical 10-25% to 40-100% on certain product categories. Importers who relied on LC margin financing to manage working capital discovered their banks could not issue new LCs without full or near-full cash collateral.

**Usance tenor restrictions tightened.** Deferred payment tenors beyond 180 days were restricted or eliminated for luxury goods, non-essential consumer products, and products Bangladesh Bank identified as import-demand-side contributors to reserve depletion. Importers who had structured supply contracts around 360-day deferred terms faced contract restructuring.

**Advance payment thresholds revised.** The per-consignment threshold for advance payment remittance without LC was revised, with stricter documentation requirements for larger advance payments. Importers making advance payments above certain thresholds now require additional documentation from their bank, including supplier verification and import purpose disclosure.

**Bangladesh Bank's BAFEDA (Bangladesh Foreign Exchange Dealer's Association) guidance:** BAFEDA guidance issued through 2024-2025 provided banks with more detailed instructions on priority allocation of foreign exchange for different import categories. Essential goods (food, medicine, petroleum, agricultural inputs) received priority allocation; discretionary consumer goods faced allocation queuing. Importers in affected categories experienced delays in LC confirmation from correspondent banks — a problem that originates not with the importer's Bangladesh bank but with the foreign bank confirming the LC.

## The Documents That Matter

For any LC-based import, Bangladesh Bank requires that the LC is opened against a valid Import Registration Certificate (IRC) specific to the importer and the product category. Importers whose IRC does not cover the product they are importing face LC refusal before the process begins.

Beyond the IRC, the document chain for a standard commercial import includes:

- **LCA (LC Authorization) form:** The bank's internal authorization for the LC, cross-referencing the import against the IRC and Bangladesh Bank requirements
- **Indent (proforma invoice):** The supplier's offer document confirming product specifications, quantity, price, and payment terms
- **Bill of Lading:** The shipping document confirming goods on board — the LC's primary trigger for payment
- **Certificate of Origin:** Required for preferential duty treatment (SAFTA, GSP) and often required by Bangladesh Bank for country-of-origin verification on certain imports
- **Insurance certificate:** Most LCs require CIF insurance naming the importer's bank as beneficiary

Missing or defective documents trigger LC discrepancies — formal notifications from the paying bank to the presenting bank that the documentation set does not comply with the LC terms. Discrepant presentations can delay payment, force renegotiation with the supplier, and in worst cases, result in the LC being refused and the shipment stranded.

## What Trips Up Importers Repeatedly

**IRC category mismatch.** The IRC specifies permitted import categories. An importer whose IRC covers "food commodities" attempting to import industrial equipment will find the LC refused. IRC maintenance — keeping the registration current, broadening categories as the business grows — is an ongoing operational requirement.

**Supplier country changes without documentation update.** An importer who previously sourced from India (SAFTA-documented, Form D COO) switching to a Chinese supplier without updating their LC terms and banking documentation creates mismatches in document presentation.

**Advance payment limits exceeded informally.** Some importers manage multiple small advance payments that collectively exceed Bangladesh Bank's per-supplier or per-year limits, without realizing the aggregate exposure. When banks run compliance checks, these accumulated remittances trigger freezes on further outflows.

**Deferred payment terms not confirmed with bank in advance.** Importers who agree usance LC terms with overseas suppliers — 180 days, 270 days — without first confirming their bank can open the LC on those terms create supply chain commitments they cannot fulfill. Banks have been increasingly restrictive on usance tenor approvals, and a bank that cannot issue the LC on the agreed terms leaves the importer in breach of the purchase contract.

## The Role of the Authorized Dealer Bank

An importer's banking relationship is not interchangeable. Authorized Dealer (AD) banks — those licensed by Bangladesh Bank to deal in foreign exchange — have different levels of correspondent bank networks, different foreign exchange allocation positions, and different internal risk appetite for LC issuance in stressed market conditions.

An importer whose bank has a strong correspondent network and a good Bangladesh Bank relationship will find LCs confirmed faster and with fewer complications than an importer whose bank operates at the edges of its correspondent capacity.

For importers making large or complex cross-border payments, the choice of banking partner — and maintaining strong relationships with the bank's trade finance team — is not an administrative detail. It is a direct operational input into supply chain reliability.
